# Attracting and Selecting Talent - Practical Guide

Recruitment for legal practices. Stated as at 8 September 2026.

---

## 1. Why this matters more than it looks

**Other than an office and some photocopiers, the only thing a law firm has is the people in it.** That is where the value sits.

**The cost of getting it wrong is 50% to 200% of the salary.** That is not just recruitment cost. It is downtime, the good people who leave because of the bad hire, the clients you lose, and the disruption.

**Everyone has worked with a lemon** - the person who is painful, who does not play as a team, who drives everyone to distraction. One is genuinely destructive in a small firm. The purpose of a proper process is to reduce your chances of hiring one.

### 1.1 The market

| | |
|---|---|
| **40 days** | Average time to hire |
| **$24,000** | Average cost to hire |
| **~4%** | Average salary growth for lawyers, against ~3.4% across other industries |
| **~4.4%** | Unemployment - historically still low |
| **~1.4 million** | Projected unfilled jobs in Australia |
| **54%** | of HR leaders expect headcount to grow |

**The number that should focus your attention.** Seek publishes a regular survey of the hardest-to-fill roles across every industry in Australia, from traffic controllers to heart surgeons. **Ten of the top twenty hardest-to-fill roles are legal roles.**

**It remains a candidate-short market.** Areas of demand: family, transactional work, banking and finance, property (cooled somewhat), insurance (perennial), commercial litigation, employment, and some insolvency.

**Which means two things.** Hiring is competitive and slow, so your process has to be sharp. And **keeping your good people matters more than finding new ones.**

## 2. Determine what you actually need

**Step one is deciding whether you need to hire at all.** See the leverage material in the **Practice Management** guide - staffing for the trough rather than the peak, and using contractors before employees.

### 2.1 The position description

Draft it before you do anything else, and split it into **essential** and **desirable** criteria aligned with firm strategy.

**Essential criteria** are the things where, if the candidate does not have them, you cannot talk to them. For a two-to-four-year construction lawyer, for example:

- **Law degree** - qualified or they cannot do the job
- **Post-qualification experience** - at least two years
- **Working rights** - non-negotiable
- **Relevant experience** in the actual practice area

**Desirable criteria** are where you think about the lemons you have worked with and what they lacked:

- **Communication** - the ability to communicate effectively with whatever the audience is, verbally and in writing. In a construction practice, being able to talk to people from a trades background as readily as to executives
- **Cultural fit** - autonomous, easy-going, patient, whatever your firm actually needs. As one participant put it: **you can train anybody anything, but culture is difficult. They either have it or they do not**
- **Diligent, organised, efficient**
- **Specific capabilities your client base requires** - a second language where you act for a particular community, cross-cultural skills where you act for Aboriginal clients

**The position description does double duty.** You will use it again when assessing applications, when structuring interview questions, and when doing reference checks. Write it once, properly.

### 2.2 A fair objection

"At the moment it sounds like every job description ever." That is correct, and it is fine. **The position description is your internal shopping list. It is not the advertisement.** The two documents have different jobs.

## 3. Sourcing

| Channel | Notes |
|---|---|
| **Word of mouth** | **The best channel.** People will not refer you a lemon because their own reputation is attached. A referral almost always produces a better candidate than a stranger responding to an advertisement |
| **Advertising** | Seek, Indeed, LinkedIn |
| **Social media** | Post on LinkedIn or Facebook that you are looking for a particular skill set |
| **Alumni associations** | |
| **People you have met across a deal** | If you saw a good lawyer on the other side of a transaction, approach them |
| **Law Society committees** | Find the committee lists and attend the functions. Better still, **if you have worked with someone on a policy submission you have seen how they think, the depth of their knowledge, and whether they can communicate to different audiences** - without ever having worked with them |
| **Third party agencies** | |

**The recruiter's own method is simply industrialised word of mouth**: reaching out and asking who do you know, then working the concentric circles outward.

## 4. Drafting the advertisement

### 4.1 Know who you are writing for

A two-to-four-year lawyer is most likely in their mid to late twenties. **They have had a bad day, they are on the bus or train, and they are scrolling Seek on a phone.** There are thousands of listings.

**Yours has to be attention grabbing, and it must not be vanilla.**

### 4.2 The headline is the exception

Job boards work on **keyword search**. Nobody types "construction project superstar" into a search box.

**Make the headline boring and searchable: "Lawyer, Projects, Sydney".** Then make everything after it attention grabbing.

### 4.3 What's in it for me

The governing principle. **The advertisement is not the shopping list.** A candidate reading a list of your requirements feels nothing.

**The advertisement is about the reader and how they will feel.** Ask yourself directly: why would someone come and work here? Then:

- **Attention grabbing**
- **WIIFM** - what's in it for me
- **Appeal to emotions and tell a story**
- **List benefits**
- **Call to action**

**In practice this means identifying your genuine advantages.** For a new sole practice that might be: not billable hours in the traditional sense, work that runs more like secondment; a genuinely good office; direct exposure to the principal rather than being two emails from a supervising partner; unusual technical exposure from the businesses around you.

### 4.4 Do not overstate

**Do not claim flexibility if your idea of flexibility is allowing jeans when they come in on a weekend.** You will be found out, and you will lose them within the year. **Be transparent.**

## 5. What candidates actually want

### 5.1 The trade-off data

An AFR survey asked what people would want in a new job **even if it meant a pay cut**. The top three came very close together:

1. **Better work/life balance**
2. **More enjoyable work**
3. **A greater sense of fulfilment**

Then: a stronger chance to grow in the role; more flexibility to work onsite or remote; more inspiring company leadership; the chance to be your whole self at work; a better relationship with boss and team.

**Work out which of those you can genuinely offer, and lead with those.**

### 5.2 Money is not the only lever

A concrete example from a panel of high-performing Gen Z professionals. One was offered **$100,000 for five days in the office, or $90,000 for three days in the office and two from home**. He took the $90,000.

**The reasoning is not laziness.** Someone accountable for every six minutes of their day, who lives far out, is trading $10,000 for an extra hour and a half to two hours a day not spent on a bus or train. At that stage of life, the time is worth more.

### 5.3 The return-to-office problem

**The moment a recruiter tells a candidate the role requires more days in the office, interest drops by around half.** People have adjusted their lives around flexibility, and in a candidate-short market, removing it is expensive.

If you require office attendance, know what it is costing you in candidate pool and decide whether the trade is worth it.

### 5.4 Benefits worth considering

- **Performance bonuses** - often linked to billings, for example eligibility once you hit three times base salary
- **Work from home / hybrid** - now effectively standard
- **Flexible hours**
- **Additional annual leave, including unpaid** - a common request is six weeks' leave with two unpaid, to cover school holidays
- **Paid parental leave**
- **Sign-on and retention bonuses**
- **Salary packaging allowances** - mobile, parking
- **EAPs, free legal services, CSR initiatives**
- **Training, professional development, memberships**
- **Employee share plans** - and for an ILP, the option of equity at the appropriate stage

**A caution on counter-offer inflation.** In the post-COVID market one candidate on $120,000 was offered $130,000 elsewhere and was counter-offered repeatedly by his own firm until he was on $200,000 - same job, same desk, same boss. **The market has since corrected.** Do not build your salary structure on the assumptions of that period.

### 5.5 What actually drives candidate choice

Seek's own data on drivers, in descending order: **work-life balance**, then **salary and compensation** (the two clearly ahead of everything else), then **career development**, then organisation, management, working environment, job location, job security and market position, colleagues, company reputation, corporate social responsibility, and company size.

## 6. Assessing applications

### 6.1 The system

**Create a system: go versus no go.** Refer back to your selection criteria.

**Do not delay. Candidates are perishable goods.** In a candidate-short market, a good applicant who does not hear from you for ten days has three other processes running.

**Interview three to five as a manageable shortlist.**

### 6.2 The three piles

Faced with thirty CVs:

| Pile | What it is |
|---|---|
| **A pile** | Meets the criteria you set out |
| **B pile** | **Does not tick all the boxes - but there is something about them** |
| **C pile** | Nowhere close. The mechanical engineering graduate from overseas who wants to be a lawyer in Australia because the weather is nice |

### 6.3 The B pile is the interesting one

**The B pile is where you find people the A pile misses**, and it is built on what the candidate does outside the law.

**What a hobby or sport actually tells you.** Someone who played rugby or cricket at a competitive level did not turn up five minutes before the game. They trained hard, showed discipline, got up on mornings when they would much rather not have, and did the same drill repeatedly. If they went in for tackles, that says something about courage.

**Swimmers are the recruiter's favourite example.** Getting up at 5am in the depths of winter to do laps of a freezing pool tells you something about work ethic that no CV summary does.

**Not every swimmer makes a great lawyer.** But you can make reasonable inferences, and those inferences are about the things you cannot train.

**Trade or hands-on background** is directly relevant in a construction practice. Someone who was a tradesman first, or who renovates houses, or who works on cars, brings something a pure academic record does not.

**Two practical consequences:**

- **Encourage candidates to put hobbies and outside interests on their CV.** It materially helps you select.
- **Use them in the interview.** They give you the rapport-building opening, and they are the honest part of the conversation.

### 6.4 Cover letters

**Do not read much into them.** They are almost all AI-generated now. A cover letter is enough to rule someone out, not enough to rule them in. At the larger end of the market much of the screening is being done by AI in any event.

## 7. The phone screen

**Interviews can be like dating.** You meet, and within eight minutes you know it will not work, and then you have fifty minutes of small talk to endure.

**The screening call prevents that.** Ten to twenty minutes, by phone, to determine whether the candidate is worth interviewing.

### 7.1 What to cover

Work through the **essential** criteria and the threshold issues:

1. **What caught your eye about the opportunity?** This tells you their motivation, and whether they have actually read the advertisement.
2. **Confirm the essentials** - post-admission experience, relevant practice experience, admission and current practising certificate.
3. **Location.** Does the commute work?
4. **Office attendance.** State your requirement plainly.
5. **Salary.** "We are looking to pay between X and Y. Does that fit your expectations?"

### 7.2 Why salary belongs in the screen

**The last thing you want is to run a full interview process and discover at the end that the candidate wants $200,000 more than your budget.** Clarify threshold issues early. It costs one phone call.

## 8. The interview

### 8.1 Structure

- **Preferably face to face**, otherwise video, otherwise phone
- Decide **how many interviewers and how many interview rounds** before you start
- **Have questions arranged, but stay open to a conversational style**
- **Focus on talent, motivation, personality and cultural fit as well as skills and experience**

### 8.2 Open with rapport

Shake hands, offer water, and open with something low-stakes drawn from their CV - a sport, an interest, the weather, whether they found the office. **The content does not matter at all.** You are creating dialogue so that everybody relaxes.

### 8.3 The 80/20 finding

**Roughly 80% of the impression formed in an interview is non-verbal.** Only about 20% is the words.

The other 80% is presentation, confidence, tone of voice, eye contact, the handshake, whether they end sentences on a rising inflection, whether they appear to want to be there.

**Two consequences.** Be conscious of it as an interviewer, because you are assessing on that basis whether you intend to or not. And it is worth telling candidates, because good people present badly and lose roles for reasons they never learn.

### 8.4 Let them ask questions

**Always give the candidate the opportunity.** You have peppered them with questions; it is only fair.

**And note what they ask.** Self-interested questions - do I get a computer, do I get a harbour view, do I get six weeks' leave - tell you something. A question about where the firm is going and whether there is room to contribute tells you something else.

## 9. Discrimination - what you cannot ask

**This is the section to read twice, because the exposure is real and the mistake is easy.**

### 9.1 Protected categories are off limits

You may not ask about:

- **Marital status** and what a spouse does
- **Children and family responsibilities**
- **Religion**
- **Sexual orientation**
- **Union membership**
- **Political affiliation**
- And every other protected attribute

**If the candidate volunteers it, that is fine.** You cannot ask.

### 9.2 Why it matters even if you do not hire them

**Two causes of action, and both survive whichever way the decision goes:**

1. **Discrimination.**
2. **Adverse action** under the Fair Work Act.

**It does not matter whether you hire the person or not.** They can bring an action either way. For someone starting a practice, being tied up in a Fair Work matter is precisely what you cannot afford.

### 9.3 The two protections

1. **Do not go near any protected category.** Not curiosity, not small talk, not "just making conversation".
2. **Always have a second person in the interview as a witness.**

### 9.4 The line

It is a genuinely fine line between a person being interested and curious, and a person asking the wrong question. **Discipline yourself to stay well back from it.**

## 10. Behavioural competency interviewing

### 10.1 The principle

**Past behaviour indicates future performance.**

Questions start with **"Tell me about a time when..."**

They are ubiquitous, and most lawyers have answered hundreds without knowing the name.

### 10.2 Build questions from your selection criteria

**This is the method.** Take each criterion, and each characteristic you identified in the lemons you have worked with, and build a question that tests it.

- If the criterion is **communicating across audiences**: "Tell me about a time you had to deal with people from a background different to what you are used to, and how you adjusted your communication."
- If the last person **struggled with deadlines**: build a question testing how they handle competing deadlines.
- If the last person **created conflict**: build a question testing how they handle disagreement.

### 10.3 Three questions worth stealing

**"Imagine you have come home from work, kicked off your shoes, sat on the couch, and there is a big smile on your face because you had a great day. What is a good day for you?"**

**"And the flip side - when have you come home and wanted to kick the proverbial?"**

Together those two reveal motivation. In the worked example the answers were *when I picked up something that was going to get missed* and *when I lost* - which tells you the candidate is competitive and likes solving problems. Both valuable in a lawyer.

**"If I rang your peers and asked them to describe you in a few adjectives, what would they say?"**

This is the fix for a specifically Australian problem. **Australians are bad at blowing their own trumpet.** Ask someone to tell you about themselves and they will not, because they are worried about how it sounds. Watch the end of any football match: the player who just won it says it was a good team effort.

**Framing it through what other people would say gives them permission**, and you get the honest answer.

### 10.4 A follow-up worth having ready

When the candidate says something revealing, probe it.

In the worked example, the candidate said people used him as Google rather than working things out themselves. The follow-up - *when that happens, how do you deal with it?* - produced: *it depends what mood I am in. I might ask them what their answer is, or tell them I think they know this and ask what they were thinking.* Then the pressure test: *you are busy, you have a deadline, do you just give them the answer?*

**That exchange tells you about supervision capability, patience, and self-awareness in a way no criterion list does.**

## 11. Reference checking

**The single most valuable stage, and the most often skipped.**

**A candidate can be excellent at interviewing and have faked it. Somebody who worked with them for three years is much harder to fake.**

### 11.1 Selecting referees

- Choose from the referees the candidate provides
- **Ideally someone who has supervised their work recently**
- **Be careful with a current employer.** Consider making an offer **subject to references**
- **Get permission to contact them**, especially current employers
- **Ask the candidate to call their referees** and let them know to expect the call
- **Call a landline where possible**, not a mobile

### 11.2 Conduct two or three detailed checks

**Two minimum. Probe any area of concern from the interviews.**

### 11.3 What to ask

**Go back to your position description and ask against it.** That is the entire method.

Plus:

- **Verify what the candidate told you.** "David mentioned he is very good at mentoring juniors. Is that your experience?"
- **Technical legal skills**
- **Verbal communication - out of five**
- **Written communication - out of five**
- **How they get on with clients**
- **Relationship with colleagues**
- **The specific capability you need.** "One minute they will be talking to a bricklayer, the next to a construction executive. How do they switch communication styles?"
- **Reasons for leaving the job**
- **Check employment dates**

The scored questions are useful because they force a differentiated answer rather than a general endorsement.

### 11.4 The most important question

> **"Would you re-hire them?"**

**Ask it every time.** It compresses everything else into one answer.

### 11.5 What you want from the second reference

**Consistency.**

If the first referee describes an introverted, softly spoken person and the second describes someone dancing on the desk on a Friday night, that is a problem. **It does not necessarily end the process, but you must probe further.**

### 11.6 The rule when you are not sure

> **"When in doubt, go without."**

The cost of a bad hire is 50% to 200% of salary. The cost of restarting the search is forty days and $24,000. **The arithmetic favours restarting.**

## 12. Offer and acceptance

### 12.1 Move fast

**Once the preferred candidate is decided, deliver the offer orally and send the paperwork the same day if possible.**

Firms have genuinely taken five or six weeks to produce an offer letter. **Have your ducks in a row before you get to this stage** so the offer can go out immediately.

### 12.2 What the offer must articulate

- **Title**
- **Salary, including superannuation**
- **Duties**
- **Whether governed by an EBA or other conditions**

### 12.3 The counter-offer and the danger period

**It is not a done deal until they are sitting at the desk.**

The pattern: the candidate walks into the managing partner's office to resign. The managing partner says how disappointing that is, mentions that the partners were discussing a promotion only last week, asks what the new firm is offering, and says the firm will not only match it but beat it.

**The pre-emptive question, asked at interview:**

> **"Hypothetically, if we were to make an offer and your employer counter-offered, what would your reaction be?"**

**If the answer is "if the money is right, I'll stay", you know exactly how interested they are.**

### 12.4 Mind the gap

**If there is a long notice period, stay in touch with the candidate between the offer and the start date.** People change their minds, circumstances change, and a candidate who hears nothing for two months is being recruited by someone else.

## 13. Checklist

**Before you advertise**

- [ ] Confirm you actually need to hire rather than contract or reorganise
- [ ] Draft the position description with **essential** and **desirable** criteria split
- [ ] Build your criteria from the lemons you have worked with, not just from the technical requirements
- [ ] Decide your salary range before you speak to anyone
- [ ] Work out what you can **genuinely** offer on work-life balance, enjoyable work and fulfilment

**Sourcing and advertising**

- [ ] Work word of mouth first - colleagues, alumni, people you met across a deal, committee contacts
- [ ] Write a **searchable, boring headline** and an attention-grabbing body
- [ ] Write it as WIIFM, not as a shopping list
- [ ] Do not overstate flexibility or anything else

**Screening**

- [ ] Sort into A, B and C piles - and **actually read the B pile**
- [ ] Do not delay. Candidates are perishable
- [ ] Phone screen 10 to 20 minutes covering essentials, location, office days and **salary**
- [ ] Shortlist three to five

**Interviewing**

- [ ] Have a **second person present as a witness**
- [ ] Prepare behavioural questions built from your selection criteria
- [ ] Open with rapport from their CV
- [ ] **Go nowhere near a protected category**
- [ ] Give them time to ask questions, and note what they ask
- [ ] Ask the counter-offer question

**References**

- [ ] Get permission; ask the candidate to warn the referees
- [ ] Two or three detailed checks, structured on the position description
- [ ] Verify what the candidate claimed
- [ ] **Ask whether they would re-hire**
- [ ] Check consistency between referees
- [ ] **When in doubt, go without**

**Offer**

- [ ] Offer orally and send paperwork the same day
- [ ] Title, salary including super, duties, applicable conditions
- [ ] Stay in touch through the notice period
