# People Management and Effective Supervision - Practical Guide

Getting performance, engagement and retention from the people you have hired. Stated as at 8 September 2026.

---

## 1. The three things that produce good outcomes with people

1. **Select for talent**
2. **Define the right outcomes**
3. **Manage appropriately**

Selection is covered in the **Attracting and Selecting Talent** guide. This guide is about the other two, which is where most firms lose the value they paid for at recruitment.

## 2. The economics of an employee

Before anything else, understand what you have actually bought.

### 2.1 The contribution curve

**A new employee costs more than they contribute in their first year.** Expecting an early return is not realistic for anyone except a genuinely lateral hire.

**Two things follow.**

**Tenure matters.** The longer you hold someone, the better the return. Nobody expects a job for life any more, so **the realistic goal is extension**. Someone who would have left after one year, given a compelling reason to stay for two, is a win. Someone who would have left after two, stretched to three, is a win.

**Steepen the learning curve.** How much you can do this depends on the area of law. **Commodity work should have a much steeper curve and you can break even considerably earlier.** Complex, bespoke work takes longer.

### 2.2 Forget the three times salary rule

**There is a lazy rule of thumb in the law that a lawyer should generate three times their salary. It is wrong at both ends.**

| Experience | Realistic multiple |
|---|---|
| **Graduate to 2 years** | Below break-even, then approaching it |
| **3 to 5 years** | **Up to around 3.5 times salary** |
| **5 to 7 years** | **Around 2 times** would be a good outcome |
| **7 years and beyond** | **Around 1.7 times** would be a good outcome |

### 2.3 Why three to five years is the sweet spot

**That is where the greatest arbitrage exists between what someone costs you and what they deliver.** After that the gap narrows.

The mechanism is worth understanding:

- **The first lift** comes from **hours getting traction** - the work they do becomes meaningful and genuinely billable.
- **The second lift** comes from **rate**, which eventually caps out.
- **Meanwhile their cost base keeps rising.**

That is the whole explanation for why every advertisement in the profession seeks "a dynamic team player with three to five years' experience", and why nobody advertises for a moody loner.

### 2.4 Area of law matters

Some areas distort the picture. In personal injury and compensation work, depending on the year and when matters settle, **lawyers can produce five times their salary**. That is circumstantial rather than structural, but it feeds directly into bonus expectations and what is perceived as fair.

**Set budgets by experience level and practice area. Not by a rule of thumb.**

## 3. Defining the right outcomes

### 3.1 The problem this solves

Ask lawyers in a firm whether they know what is expected of them and you will often get "not really, but no news is good news".

**That charming naivety is not long for this world.** The lawyers who perform well have clear expectations.

### 3.2 The three inputs

A firm needs three things to succeed:

```
        DOLLARS
       /       \
   STAFF  ---  CLIENTS
```

**There is competitive tension across all three:**

| Tension | How it resolves |
|---|---|
| The firm wants to pay staff as little as possible; staff want to be paid as much as possible | Market forces and employment agreements |
| Staff, at the extreme, want to do as little work as possible; clients want them to go over and above | Scoping and fee agreements |
| Clients want to pay as little as possible; the firm wants them to pay as much as possible | Fee agreements |

**The critical consequence: the firm will operate at the level of the least-performing of the three.**

If the firm is cash constrained, there are limits on what you can pay. You fall behind in the compensation arms race. You lose staff. Your ability to service clients slips. The money coming in from clients falls. **And around you go.**

**So performance criteria must contain elements of all three.** There is no point lifting one and letting the other two wither.

### 3.3 The exercise - do this for your own firm

Under each of the three headings, write down **activities** that would enhance the outcome. **Activities, not goals.**

- **An activity** is "capture all time".
- **A goal** is "hit six hours a day". That is not what you want here.

And write down what **would** enhance each input, regardless of whether you currently do it.

**A real list, generated by practitioners in the session:**

| **Dollars (fees and profit)** | **Staff (morale and engagement)** | **Clients (satisfaction)** |
|---|---|---|
| Niche market focus | Work-life balance | Communication |
| Accurate quoting - neither under nor over | Work from home | Transparency |
| Uplift | Good bonus or incentive structure | Building the relationship rather than keeping it transactional |
| Consistent billing | Adequate resources | Asking for feedback to improve next time |
| Low write-offs | Giving ownership - of matters and of the firm | Efficiency |
| Chasing debtors | Mentoring | Taking ownership of mistakes |
| Reviewing trust deposit timing | Monitoring capacity | Professionalism |
| | Progression planning | |
| | Two-way communication | |

**If everyone in your firm did all of those things, you would make plenty of money, have engaged staff, and have happy clients.**

### 3.4 The measurement problem

Now ask which of those you **actively measure**. Not which you could measure. Which you do.

The honest answer: **time, billings, write-offs, quoting, debtor days, trust compliance.** Perhaps mentoring, sometimes.

**Of a list of roughly thirty items, most firms measure about a third - and all of it sits in the dollars column.**

That is the entire explanation for why staff and client outcomes drift. You get what you measure.

### 3.5 Building performance criteria

**Draw up the list for your firm. Then select from it when setting performance criteria - and make sure the selection is rounded across all three inputs.**

**If something is important, find a measure for it.** Some things are binary and that is enough: they wrote the article or they did not; they did the mentoring session or they did not.

### 3.6 Divide the day

A practical structure:

| Category | Example |
|---|---|
| **Fee generation** | Five and a half hours, with the associated dollars measures |
| **Firm time** | Investment back into the firm |
| **Me time** | Their own - the gym, a shower, whatever they need |
| **Staff activities** | A defined allocation, say five hours per week across staff and client work |
| **Client activities** | Measurable where possible |

### 3.7 Sculpt it for the individual

**Under three years, you overwhelmingly want them to become good at their job.** Put them on fee generation. **Do not make marketing a priority for a junior.**

If they want to do more, and they are already performing well, then bring them in: short articles, blogs, and client contact if they are genuinely the sort of person who connects well.

**But do not force structured client catch-ups on a junior for whom it is simply not their thing.**

### 3.8 How performance criteria go wrong

Two worked examples from practitioners, both worth holding in mind.

**The impossible structure.** A firm that required hurdle billings, no debtors, funds in trust for every client before taking the client on, **and** five and a half billable hours a day. You could not achieve everything you were expected to achieve. Then a quarterly performance review with a "please explain".

**Malicious compliance.** A firm that gave clients a customer satisfaction score at the end of a matter. **Staff stopped calling clients they thought were difficult, and passed them to more junior staff so that the juniors wore the negative scores.**

**Approach any performance measure with a cynical mindset.** Ask what behaviour it will actually produce, not what it is supposed to produce.

**And a third, on cultural assumptions.** A practitioner had "attracting work from the Chinese market" built into his KPIs because the partners assumed a cultural connection, when his family had been in Australia for two hundred years and had stopped speaking Chinese a century ago. It was removed. **Do not build criteria on assumptions about a person's background.** See also the protected categories material in the **Attracting and Selecting Talent** guide.

## 4. Managing appropriately

### 4.1 The open door policy is not management

Partners routinely boast about having an open door policy. **It is not management.**

**What an open door policy actually says is: come and interrupt me if you dare.** You are putting the decision in the hands of the person with the least power in the relationship.

Two outcomes follow. Either the person lacks the self-awareness and interrupts constantly, or - far more likely - **they sit at their desk thinking they had better not, because she will think they are useless, and they churn away wasting time.**

**Use an open door policy as a supplement to a structured approach. On its own it is hopeless.**

### 4.2 Situational leadership - the four maturity levels

A working adaptation of the Hersey-Blanchard situational leadership model. **Everyone travels from M1 to M4, and your role changes at each stage.**

| Level | Ability | Confidence | Your role | What that sounds like |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **M1** | Low | Low | **Director** | "This is what I want you to do. This is how I want you to do it. Go and do it. When it is done, come back and show me" |
| **M2** | Growing | Rising - often ahead of ability | **Guide** | "What are you working on? How do you think it should be progressed?" You check their approach, offer ideas, then let them go |
| **M3** | High | May dip momentarily as the training wheels come off | **Coach and encourager** | "You have got this. How do you plan on doing it? That is great, go and do it. I am here if you need me" |
| **M4** | High | High | **Delegate** | Hand it over |

**Note the confidence dynamic.** Confidence tends to run ahead of ability, particularly in young men. Then at M3, when you step back, confidence can dip before it recovers.

### 4.3 The failure mode

**Under time pressure, law firms treat an M1 like an M4.** They hand the work over and expect it to come back done.

That is the origin of a large proportion of the supervision failures set out in the **Risk Management** guide, including the cases where the firm breached its duty of care to the client.

### 4.4 Meeting cadence

| Level | Frequency | Length | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| **M1 and M2** | **Daily** | 15 to 20 minutes | Their matters and whether they are progressing |
| **M1 and M2** | **Weekly** | | Their **performance** and whether they are on track |
| **M3 and M4** | **Weekly** | 15 to 20 minutes | Performance |

**If you have set a hard floor - minimum acceptable performance of five hours a day rather than a lofty ceiling nobody reaches - you must meet daily and hold them accountable weekly to achieve it.** The floor without the cadence is just a number on a page.

**And keep the meetings.** Repeatedly setting and cancelling a meeting with a junior tells them they are not important. See section 8.6 of the **Risk Management** guide.

## 5. Where to spend your time

### 5.1 The distribution

Think of a bell curve, one standard deviation either side of the mean:

| Share | Category |
|---|---|
| **~15%** | **Stars** |
| **~70%** | **Good solid performers** |
| **~15%** | **Duds** |

Most small firms do not have enough people to produce a real distribution. **Keep the concept anyway.**

### 5.2 Where you actually spend it, and where you should

| | Typical allocation | Better allocation |
|---|---|---|
| **Duds** | 70% | 5% |
| **Solid performers** | 25% | 25% |
| **Stars** | 5% | 70% |

**Reverse the percentages.**

**The reasoning: a dud will be a dud.** You hover above them with a big stick to get them over the line, and the moment you turn away they revert. **Investment in duds does not produce a return.**

**Stars have more in them.** If you have managed to land a genuinely good person, invest your time in them. Do not ignore them because they do not need you.

### 5.3 What juniors actually want

**The one thing junior lawyers consistently say they want more of is access to senior people.**

**It is not your sparkling personality. It is what they can learn from you.**

That is also the cheapest retention lever available to a small firm, and the one most often neglected because senior time feels expensive.

## 6. Compensation and bonuses

### 6.1 A tiered approach

| Group | Approach |
|---|---|
| **Duds and the bottom half of solid performers** | Pay them what they are worth to you |
| **The top half of solid performers** | Take market into account |
| **Stars** | Consider a **bonus component** |

### 6.2 The blunt finding on bonuses

**No bonus structure has been observed taking a dud to a star in the law. Lawyers tend not to be motivated that way.**

**So if you put a bonus structure in place, design it to compensate the stars.** Do not put one in expecting it to fundamentally change the behaviour of underperformers and drag them to the light. It will not.

**And many bonus structures are actively toxic.** Two examples from the session:

- **The unreachable hurdle.** Hit six and a half billable hours a day, and only then receive 10% of anything above it - **calculated after write-offs**. Not motivating. It produces resentment.
- **The malicious compliance case** at section 3.8.

### 6.3 What people reported actually working

**A qualitative discretionary bonus.** One firm structured its bonus around qualitative rather than quantitative factors - recognising that where someone cannot control incoming work or billables, the traditional measures are unfair. Imperfect, but reasonable.

**No bonus at all, and excellent compensation.** The arrangement reported as working best had **no bonus structure**. The firm looks at the top of the market, goes to its employees, and says: we are bumping you up. **No employee has ever had to ask for a pay rise or sit and fight for one.** Employee satisfaction is described as through the roof.

**That is worth sitting with.** The administrative simplicity, the removal of an annual adversarial conversation, and the signal it sends may be worth more than any incentive design.

## 7. Engagement and the right measuring stick

### 7.1 What the research says

The Gallup research findings that underpin this area:

- **There is a link between employee opinion and practice group performance** - and that link runs to **productivity, profitability, staff retention and client satisfaction**.
- **Opinions are formed by immediate managers rather than by overall firm policies or procedures.**

**That second finding is the operative one.** Your firm-wide policies matter far less than what the supervising partner does day to day. You cannot policy your way out of a bad manager.

### 7.2 An appropriate measuring stick

The useful questions are those that **the most engaged people answer positively and everyone else answers negatively or neutrally**. See the nine non-financial predictors in the **Practice Management** guide, which do exactly this job.

Covered fully in the **Attracting and Selecting Talent** guide. The points that belong here:

**The alignment that has to hold:**

> **Firm strategy → People needs → People proposition**

**Hire for attitude and train for skill.**

**Match the interview with the position**, and give **realistic job previews**. Overselling the role produces a resignation within twelve months.

## 9. Developing talent

Four elements:

**Appraisal and review.** Structured, on a cadence, against the criteria you actually set.

**Focus on strengths, as much as is practical.** Developing what someone is already good at produces more than remediating what they are not.

**Use of non-billable time.** This is where firm time and staff time from section 3.6 become real. If non-billable time is not allocated and protected, development does not happen.

**Holding partners accountable.** The one most firms avoid. If a partner does not supervise, does not run the file reviews, and cancels the meetings, **that has to have consequences** - or none of the rest of this operates. See the **Partnership Management** guide.

## 10. Retention

Two distinct strategies with different time horizons. **You need both, and they are not interchangeable.**

| **Make them want to stay** | **Stop them leaving** |
|---|---|
| **Long-term impact** | **Short-term impact** |
| Emotional bonds - vision, values, leadership | Create tangible bonds - bonuses, benefits tied to tenure |
| Challenging opportunities | Increase salaries |
| Viable career paths | |
| Open access to information and decision-making | |

**The left column is what actually holds people.** The right column buys time.

**A firm that relies only on the right column is in an arms race it cannot win**, because there is always a firm with more money. And as the recruitment data shows, the top three reasons people would change jobs even at a pay cut are work-life balance, more enjoyable work and a greater sense of fulfilment - **all of which live in the left column**.

## 11. Supervision and the conduct obligation

Everything above sits inside a regulatory obligation.

**A solicitor with designated responsibility for a matter must exercise reasonable supervision over solicitors and all other employees engaged in providing the legal services for that matter** ([ASCR r 37](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.37)).

**Each principal must ensure reasonable steps are taken** so that legal practitioner associates comply with their obligations ([LPUL s 34](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/act-2014-16a#sec.34)), and a principal is **taken to have contravened** a provision the practice breached where they were in a position to influence and failed to take reasonable steps ([LPUL s 35](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/act-2014-16a#sec.35)).

The full treatment, including the supervision structure, file review discipline, remote supervision and the disciplinary cases, is in the **Risk Management** guide at section 8. The essential points:

- **Reasonable supervision is much more than an open-door policy.** It requires appropriate systems, clear communication, and support and structure.
- **What is reasonable varies** with the employee's experience, qualifications and role, and with the type and complexity of the work: *Legal Services Commissioner v Michael Vincent Baker* [2005] LPT 002.
- **Monitor individual workloads.** Two disciplinary cases turned on exhaustion rather than character - a solicitor absorbing everyone else's files who lied to a client, and a graduate who collapsed at work from exhaustion and then falsified a document.
- **Build a culture where "I made a mistake" and "I am stuck" can be said without cost.** Berating the first person to say it guarantees the next person stays silent.
- **High staff turnover is a risk indicator**, and in small firms it usually indicates a culture of churn.
- **Being an excellent rainmaker is not the same as being a good people manager.** Question why someone who is not is being put near people management.

**And note the wellbeing overlay.** Since 1 October 2022 the [WHS Regulation 2017](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2017-0404) has imposed an express duty to manage **psychosocial risks**, and the hazards it names include job demands, low job control, poor support, lack of role clarity, and inadequate reward and recognition. **Those are the same things this guide is about.** See the **Stress Management** guide.

## 12. Checklist

**Defining outcomes**

- [ ] Run the dollars / staff / clients exercise for your own firm and produce the activity list
- [ ] Identify which of those activities you **actually measure** today
- [ ] Build performance criteria that draw from **all three** columns, not just dollars
- [ ] Find a measure for anything you have decided is important, even a binary one
- [ ] Divide the day explicitly into fee generation, firm time, staff time and client time
- [ ] Sculpt the criteria for the individual - keep juniors on fee generation
- [ ] **Stress-test every measure for the behaviour it will actually produce**

**Managing**

- [ ] Assess each person's maturity level: M1, M2, M3 or M4
- [ ] Adjust your role accordingly - director, guide, coach, delegate
- [ ] Set the meeting cadence: daily for M1 and M2, weekly for everyone on performance
- [ ] **Keep the meetings.** Do not repeatedly cancel
- [ ] Stop describing an open door policy as management

**Time and money**

- [ ] Audit where your time actually goes across duds, solid performers and stars
- [ ] **Reverse it.** Invest in the stars
- [ ] Give juniors access to senior people - it is what they want and it is cheap
- [ ] If you have a bonus structure, confirm it is designed to reward stars, not to reform duds
- [ ] Consider whether excellent compensation with no bonus would serve you better

**Retention**

- [ ] Audit your retention approach across "make them want to stay" and "stop them leaving"
- [ ] If you are only operating in the second column, fix it
- [ ] Confirm you can articulate a viable career path for each person
- [ ] Allocate and protect non-billable development time
- [ ] Hold partners accountable for supervision, or nothing else here works
