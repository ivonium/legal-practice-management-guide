# Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing - Practical Guide

For NSW legal practices. Law and guidance stated as at 8 September 2026.

---

## 1. Where things stand

The obligations are **live**. The course deck was prepared while the regime was still bedding in; this guide states the position as it now operates.

| Date | What happened |
|---|---|
| 10 December 2024 | *Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Amendment Act 2024* (Cth) received assent |
| 7 January 2025 | *Financial Transaction Reports Act 1988* (Cth) repealed. The old solicitors' significant cash transaction reporting regime ended |
| 31 March 2026 | Reformed obligations commenced for tranche 1 entities and virtual asset service providers. **Enrolment opened for tranche 2** |
| **1 July 2026** | **AML/CTF obligations commenced for tranche 2 entities, including law practices** |
| **29 July 2026** | Outer deadline to enrol with AUSTRAC and notify your AML/CTF compliance officer |
| 31 March 2027 | First annual compliance report expected due. **Verify the reporting period and due date with AUSTRAC**, as published guidance has been inconsistent on whether the first period is a calendar year or 1 July 2026 to 30 June 2027 |

**The one-line summary.** If your practice provides a designated service, you are a reporting entity. You must have enrolled, appointed a compliance officer, completed a risk assessment and an AML/CTF program, be doing customer due diligence, be monitoring transactions, and be ready to report.

**AUSTRAC is already enforcing.** Notices are being issued to entities that failed to enrol on time, reportedly in the order of tens of thousands of dollars per day of lateness. Enrolment failures are the easiest breach for a regulator to detect, which is exactly why they are being pursued first.

## 2. Why lawyers are in the regime

### 2.1 The international driver

AML/CTF standards are set globally by the **Financial Action Task Force (FATF)**, through its 40 Recommendations plus 9 special recommendations on terrorism financing, adopted in more than 200 jurisdictions. The key pillars are criminalisation and asset seizure, preventive measures (CDD, record keeping, suspicious activity reporting), beneficial ownership transparency, supervision and enforcement, international cooperation, and targeted financial sanctions.

**Australia was one of the last jurisdictions in the world to bring professional services into the regime.** FATF conducts mutual evaluations and can **grey list** a country that does not meet the standards. Grey listing carries significant economic consequences, undermines international trust in the financial system and damages national reputation. Australia has not performed well historically in mutual evaluations, and the assumption inside the AML community that Australia would never actually be grey listed turned out to be complacent. That is why tranche 2 happened and why AUSTRAC is taking supervision seriously.

**Nothing about the Australian regime is unusual.** With minor technical variation, the same framework operates in New Zealand, the UK, Hong Kong and across Europe. What *is* unusual is the regulator.

### 2.2 AUSTRAC's dual role

AUSTRAC is both Australia's **AML/CTF regulator** and its **financial intelligence unit**. In most jurisdictions those two roles sit in separate bodies, which slows detection of non-compliance.

Because AUSTRAC does both, it can cross-reference what it knows about a sector's weaknesses against what is actually being reported, and identify the gaps. If AUSTRAC knows a category of entity has weak systems, it can look at what is coming through, or failing to come through, and act.

AUSTRAC is also among the **harshest AML supervisors globally** on penalty size.

### 2.3 Why professional services

The often-quoted 1996 line about accountancy firms captures it:

> Money laundering is not conceived by wicked individuals in some Dickensian den of crimes. It is planned, executed, minuted and concealed in clean, respectable, warm and well-lit city centre offices, by quiet men and women in smart clothes who do not raise their voices and keep a relatively low profile.

The mechanical point: **without a professional involved, money typically has to be layered three to five times before it appears clean. With a professional in front of or inside the transaction, one or two layers is enough.**

That is the whole reason lawyers are captured. You compress the laundering cycle.

The global estimate for laundered funds is **AUD 2 to 8 trillion annually**, which would make it the second largest market in the world.

### 2.4 AUSTRAC's assessment of lawyers

The national risk assessment rates lawyers as posing a **high and stable** money laundering vulnerability. "High" because you can integrate funds very quickly. "Stable" because the way lawyers operate is predictable and does not change rapidly, unlike, say, virtual asset providers.

The key drivers AUSTRAC identifies:

- Lawyers can facilitate money laundering, **including unwittingly**, through the provision of professional services.
- Law enforcement consistently identifies criminals seeking and exploiting the advice and services of lawyers to legitimise activity and obfuscate proceeds of crime.
- Domestic criminals rely on lawyers, often working alongside accountants, financial advisers and offshore service providers, to conceal illicit funds and beneficial ownership.
- The key vulnerabilities are **criminal use of law firm trust accounts**, **facilitation of real estate transactions**, and **creation and administration of legal structures**.

**Note the three vulnerabilities.** They map directly onto ordinary small-firm practice: your trust account, your conveyancing, and your company and trust setups.

### 2.5 High harm and low harm

Not in the legislation, but consistently observable in enforcement outcomes: **where non-compliance is linked to high harm crime, the proceedings go to penalty almost every time.** Where the underlying conduct is low harm, remedial action or a formal notice is more common.

| Low harm | High harm |
|---|---|
| Crime society dislikes, with a victim, but where recovery or remediation is possible and money can largely fix it: tax evasion and tax crime, counterfeit currency, certain frauds | Crime with a clear victim and widespread, sometimes multigenerational, consequences: narcotics manufacture and supply, human trafficking, child sexual exploitation, hostage taking, terrorism |

Australia's leading predicate crimes are **illicit drugs, tax and revenue crime, government-funded program fraud, scams and illicit tobacco**. Child sexual exploitation and human trafficking remain low volume but are increasing.

### 2.6 The case that should focus attention

**Operation Elbrus**, a joint ATO and AFP investigation into large-scale tax fraud and money laundering involving more than $105 million defrauded from the ATO. **Two lawyers were convicted.**

- A **partner at a law firm** used his access to the practice's **trust account** to knowingly launder over **$24 million** in criminal proceeds, creating a false document trail using straw directors to "authorise" trust distributions.
- A **commercial and taxation lawyer and accountant**, a partner at a different firm, became a critical trusted adviser to the principal conspirators, using his legal and accounting skills and **his status as a solicitor** to facilitate laundering and tax fraud.

Those were knowing participants. But the regime exists mainly for the **unknowing and exploited** practitioner, who is the far larger category.

**The pattern to watch for in yourself:** a practice grows dependent on a single client, or your billings and career progression become tied to one relationship. Red flags appear. You are already in too deep, and you keep going.

## 3. How the laundering works

You need to understand the mechanism in order to recognise it. That is the only reason it is set out here.

### 3.1 The three stages

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Placement** | Introducing physical cash into the financial system. "Cash" in AML means notes and coins, not cleared funds |
| **2. Layering** | Multiple transactions to distance the money from its origin. **This is where lawyers sit, and it is why lawyers are high risk** |
| **3. Integration** | Introducing the laundered money into the legitimate economy |

Not all three stages have to occur. Placement is becoming less common globally as crypto substitutes for cash, but because Australia's dominant predicate crime is narcotics, **large volumes of physical cash remain in circulation here**.

### 3.2 Terrorism financing runs in reverse

| Stage | What happens |
|---|---|
| **1. Raising** | From both legitimate and illegitimate sources |
| **2. Moving** | To obscure the origin and get funds to the group |
| **3. Using** | To fund operations and support |

Money laundering starts with dirty money and makes it appear clean. Terrorism financing often starts with **legitimate** money and conceals the purpose. Law practices are not especially vulnerable to TF, but you need to know it exists because the reporting timeframe is shorter.

### 3.3 The real estate typology, worked through

This is the most common way a law practice gets used, and it is worth following in full because it shows how invisible each individual step looks.

Two participants in a drug syndicate need to move money between themselves without any traceable link, because a direct bank transfer would flag them as associates.

1. **Participant A buys a modest property using genuine, explicable funds.** A middle-aged professional buying a $1 million property in an unfashionable suburb raises no flags at all.
2. **A renovates it using cash**, paying tradespeople and buying unregulated goods such as carpet and curtains, sinking $200,000 of dirty cash into it. Fair market value is now perhaps $1.4 million.
3. **A lists it with a carefully chosen agent** - either one so busy with high-value listings they pay no attention, or one so desperate for a commission that they will never suggest slowing the deal down.
4. **It sells to Participant B for $1.6 million**, well above market value. B's dirty cash goes in; A's clean profit comes out.
5. **You act on the conveyance.** A presents as entirely low risk: a modest property, a modest profit, nothing like a headline transaction.
6. **They repeat it.** A's clean $500,000 profit funds the next deposit. Four cycles produce perhaps $2 million.
7. **A comes back to you** and says they have done well in property. You sensibly suggest **incorporating a company**. A is now a property development business, not an individual.
8. **An accountant prepares financial statements.** Now there are contracts, an incorporated entity, and audited-looking accounts.
9. **Integration is complete.** When A makes a large investment, the source of funds is documented: four property sales, sale and purchase agreements, and **funds arriving from a reputable law firm's trust account**.

**The critical point.** Banks and investment firms frequently do not scrutinise payments arriving from a law firm's trust account, because they assume checks have already been done. **Your trust account is the laundering credential.** That is the heightened risk.

**What you would be looking for:**

- **Overs and unders** - purchase or sale prices materially out of line with market value or the previous purchase price.
- A **fast-growing property portfolio** in a client whose profile does not obviously support it.
- The same counterparty recurring across transactions.
- Renovation value that does not reconcile with documented expenditure.

### 3.4 Cash-intensive businesses

The other placement pattern you may encounter acting for businesses: **smoothing**, where dirty cash is deposited alongside genuine takings so that one business appears to carry both. Look for takings inconsistent with the apparent scale of the operation.

## 4. Are you captured? Designated services

The complete list is in **section 6, Table 6 of the AML/CTF Act**. AUSTRAC has issued **Professional Services Core Guidance** on what it considers in scope and out of scope, and maintains a "check if you'll be regulated" tool.

### 4.1 The eight services relevant to legal practices

| Service | Description |
|---|---|
| **Real estate transactions** | Assisting in the planning or execution of a transaction to sell, buy or transfer real estate |
| **Sale or transfer of legal entities** | Assisting in the planning or execution of a transaction to sell, buy or transfer a body corporate or legal arrangement |
| **Management of property or assets** | Receiving, holding, controlling or managing a person's property to assist in the planning or execution of a transaction |
| **Equity or debt financing** | Assisting in organising, planning or executing a transaction for equity or debt financing relating to a body corporate or legal arrangement |
| **Shelf companies** | Selling or transferring a shelf company |
| **Entity creation or restructuring** | Assisting in the planning or execution of the creation or restructuring of a body corporate or legal arrangement |
| **Official roles** | Acting, or arranging for another person to act, on behalf of a person as a director, secretary, partner, trustee or similar position |
| **Registered office services** | Providing a registered office address or principal place of business address for a body corporate or legal arrangement |

### 4.2 Reading the definitions

**Get comfortable with grey.** The language is deliberately elastic:

- **"Assisting in" versus "acting"** - two different things, and you can be captured by either.
- **"Planning or executing"** - you can be captured for doing part of a transaction, not only all of it.
- The concept of **materially advancing** a transaction is central.

Each service sits on a continuum rather than a bright line.

### 4.3 The points that cause most confusion

**Real estate.** Wide. It includes **gifting** a property, not just sale.

**Sale or transfer of legal entities.** Internationally this has extended to the sale of a **substantial portion** of a business - if a finance company sells its entire loan book, it has effectively sold the business. Not yet spelled out in AUSTRAC guidance, but expect it.

**Entity creation or restructuring.** Setting up a trust, a joint venture or an SMSF is captured, and so is restructuring them. A share sale putting a new shareholder in place, particularly crossing the 25% threshold, will generally be captured here.

**Management of property or assets.** "Property" extends to money, key assets, bank accounts and virtual asset wallets, as well as physical assets such as fine jewellery and gold. This service has the **widest capture and the widest exclusions**. The exclusions include court-ordered payments and payments to government bodies such as the ATO. **Read the exclusions carefully** - they are on the AUSTRAC website.

**Payment of your own professional fees is excluded.** So a client's friend paying your fees in cash is not, strictly, a designated service. But see 4.5.

### 4.4 Who is your customer in a chain?

This trips people up constantly. Work the chain:

1. A **natural person** instructs you to create a company. The natural person is your customer; the service is the creation of the company.
2. You then provide **registered office services** to that company. The **company** is now your customer for that service.
3. For an SMSF: the natural person instructs the creation of a company; the company instructs creation of the trust. The pathway is **natural person, then company, then the SMSF**, possibly with additional natural persons along the way.
4. If you then manage the fund's money, the fund is your customer for that service too.

**Each designated service has its own customer.** Map the pathway before you do CDD.

### 4.5 The technically-excluded-but-obviously-suspicious problem

A client charged with an offence has a friend attend with cash to pay for the trial. Technically this falls within the professional fees exclusion and is not a designated service.

But a large cash payment is inherently high risk, and $50,000 raises very different flags from $500. There is no guidance on what to do when you are conflicted.

**The prudent course is to report.** If AUSTRAC does not need it, no harm done. The alternative, of taking the technical position and not reporting, is defensible but leaves you carrying the judgment alone.

### 4.6 Pre-commencement clients

**You do not need to conduct initial CDD on customers you provided designated services to before 1 July 2026.**

But you **do** have to comply with ongoing CDD and monitoring for them. Initial CDD becomes necessary if an SMR obligation arises, or if there is a significant change that increases the customer's ML/TF risk.

## 5. The obligation set

Practices commonly assume AML is three things: a risk assessment, a compliance program, and CDD. It is considerably more:

1. Enrol with AUSTRAC
2. Appoint an AML/CTF compliance officer
3. Complete a **money laundering / terrorism financing risk assessment**
4. Establish an **AML/CTF program** (policies, procedures, systems and controls)
5. **Governance**: senior management approval and governing body oversight
6. **Customer due diligence**: initial, ongoing, enhanced and simplified
7. **Transaction and account monitoring**
8. **Reporting to AUSTRAC**: SMRs, TTRs, annual compliance report
9. **Record keeping** (7 years)
10. **Staff training**
11. **Employee due diligence**
12. **Independent evaluation** of your program

The compliance officer is responsible for the operation of the program, but **the business as a whole carries the obligations**.

## 6. Enrolment

- Enrolment opened **31 March 2026**.
- You must enrol **within 28 days of commencing to provide a designated service**. For most practices already providing designated services on commencement, the outer deadline was **29 July 2026**.
- If you begin providing a designated service **after** that date, you have **28 days**.
- You must notify AUSTRAC of any **change to your enrolment details within 14 days**.
- **Enrol for every designated service you provide**, not just the obvious one.
- Registration is a separate and additional step that applies only to remittance network providers, independent remitters and virtual asset service providers. It does not apply to law practices.

**Obligations commenced on 1 July 2026 whether or not you enrolled.** Enrolment is not a precondition to the duties applying.

## 7. The AML/CTF compliance officer

### 7.1 Who

- Must have **authority at management level** and the resources to do the job. This is **not junior or administrative work**. Partner or practice manager level.
- Must be a **fit and proper person**.
- Must be an **Australian resident**.
- In a sole practice, you are the compliance officer, but **the formal appointment must still be made and AUSTRAC notified**.
- The AML/CTF compliance officer, the senior manager and the governing body **can all be the same person** in a small practice.

### 7.2 What they need

- Access to all relevant areas of the business
- Access to all relevant employees
- **The power to deal with any problem relating to AML/CTF obligations** - including the partner who does not want to do CDD and pushes the grey areas
- The knowledge, expertise and other resources needed

### 7.3 What they do

- Ensure compliance with the AML/CTF Act and Rules
- **Report regularly to the governing body and senior management**
- Create, implement and maintain internal policies, procedures and systems
- Act as the contact point with AUSTRAC: submitting reports, liaising on audits and requests
- Address feedback from AUSTRAC and from independent evaluations

### 7.4 You cannot outsource responsibility

You may engage an external adviser, and an external person can even be appointed as your compliance officer. **The obligation and the liability stay with the reporting entity.** Nothing sticks to the consultant.

## 8. Risk assessment

**This is a mandatory document and you cannot lawfully provide designated services without one.**

### 8.1 What it must consider

| Factor | Questions |
|---|---|
| **Designated services** | Which of the eight do you actually provide, and in what volume? |
| **Customers** | How many companies, trusts, international buyers, high net worth clients? |
| **Delivery methods** | Do you meet clients in person, or remotely? |
| **Jurisdictional exposure** | High net worth immigration work with heavy international exposure, or a domestic suburban practice? |

All assessed against your practice's **nature, size and complexity**. Small and boutique typically reduces the risk profile.

### 8.2 The starting point is high

Your sector assessment is **high risk**. You will land at high risk unless your practice is genuinely different from the norm.

**A practice that can justify a lower score:** a family law specialist with almost no captured designated services, no international clients, occasional trust creation to protect children's inheritances, a trust account holding funds during settlements, and a little conveyancing. That caseload genuinely differs from a full-service firm.

**What you cannot do** is assert low risk for a full-service firm on a one-page document. That is precisely what draws a visit.

### 8.3 Review triggers

Revisit **at least annually**, and sooner where there is a material change in the facts and circumstances of the business. A new partner specialising in mergers and acquisitions changes your risk profile immediately. So does expansion, a new practice area, or a shift in client base.

The assessment must also reflect **legislative changes and new typologies**.

### 8.4 Methodology

Must be **clear, logical and produce a meaningful assessment**. Not so complex it cannot be applied, not so simplified it produces no differentiation.

## 9. AML/CTF program

### 9.1 What it is

The program sets out the **policies, procedures, systems and controls** of your entire AML/CTF regime. It goes far beyond identifying your clients.

| Layer | Purpose |
|---|---|
| **Policies** | Set out expectations, standards and behaviours |
| **Procedures** | Day-to-day operations, processes and business rules |
| **Systems** | The actions in place to ensure the business complies with policies and procedures and modifies risk |

**The program is no longer required to be structured as Part A and Part B** following the 2024 reforms, but the substantive content requirements remain.

### 9.2 Governance

- **Senior management must approve** the program.
- The **governing body must oversee** it and take reasonable steps to ensure compliance.
- Policies must be appropriate to the practice's nature, size and complexity, and kept up to date.

**In a partnership, build formal reporting lines from the compliance officer to the partnership.** Auditors test this.

### 9.3 Why these two documents matter more than they look

The risk assessment and program are your **first layer of protection**, from criminals and from AUSTRAC.

AUSTRAC has too many entities to examine individually. It casts a wide net and picks off the easy targets: first the entities that did not enrol, then the ones whose risk assessment and program are obviously inadequate. **Entities whose first submissions are robust very rarely attract a supervisory review.** These documents function as a gate.

They are **living documents** requiring regular meaningful review, and they are technical. Creating and implementing them properly requires real AML knowledge.

**A practical shortcut for small practices:** the Law Society of NSW publishes **Starter Kits** which, for most legal practices, can be adopted and implemented as a compliant AML/CTF program.

## 10. Customer due diligence

### 10.1 What you must establish on reasonable grounds

- Take reasonable steps to establish that the **customer is who they claim to be**
- **Identify the customer's ML/TF risk**
- Collect **KYC information**
- **Verify** KYC information using reliable and independent data
- Identify **any person acting on the customer's behalf** and their authority to act
- Identify **any person on whose behalf the customer is receiving the service**
- Determine whether any of those persons is a **politically exposed person**
- Establish the **nature and purpose** of the business relationship or occasional transaction

### 10.2 Individuals

**Collect:** full name; date of birth; residential address; unique identifier.

**Verify** using either:

| Option 1 | Option 2 |
|---|---|
| A government-issued **primary photographic** ID document: current driver's licence, passport, proof of age card, foreign national identity card | A **primary non-photographic** document (birth certificate or citizenship certificate) **plus** a secondary document showing name and address, such as a utility bill |

### 10.3 Beneficial owners

A beneficial owner is an individual who directly or indirectly:

- **ultimately owns 25% or more** of your customer; **or**
- **otherwise controls** your customer.

**A customer may have more than one beneficial owner.** There are two separate tests - **ownership** and **effective control** - and you must apply both. A director with no shareholding can still be a beneficial owner through control.

Layered structures require you to trace ownership up through each layer until you reach natural persons.

### 10.4 Company clients

- Collect and verify the **full name as registered with ASIC** (or equivalent) and the **ACN** or equivalent
- Collect the **registered office address**
- Collect the **main place of business address**, if any
- If a **proprietary company**, collect the name of **each director**
- For **each beneficial owner**, collect and verify identity

### 10.5 Trust clients

- Collect and verify the **full name of the trust**
- Collect the **full business name of the trustee**, if any
- Collect and verify the **full name of all trustees**
- Collect and verify the **settlor**
- Collect the **full address of all trustees**
- Collect the **type of trust** and the **country of establishment**
- For beneficiaries, collect **either** the full name of each beneficiary, **or** if identified by reference to a class, the details of that class

### 10.6 Nature and purpose

This is the requirement most often done badly, and it is the one that makes monitoring work. It serves three functions: it lets you assess the risk of the service, it establishes a **baseline of expected activity** against which you can spot the unusual, and it lets you detect divergence during ongoing CDD.

**At client level:**

- **Who is the client?** Beyond the mandated KYC: what business are they in? Is it cash intensive? Do they operate in high-risk jurisdictions?
- **Why?** Does it make sense for this client to be engaging us for this service?
- **How often** are we expecting to be engaged?
- **How much** are we expecting to transact on their behalf?

**At matter level:** record the nature and purpose of every new matter, considering:

- **Alignment** between the new instructions and what you know about the client
- **Use of the trust account**
- **Expected volume** of transactions
- **Parties involved**, and any **third-party payer**

## 11. Enhanced customer due diligence

### 11.1 When it is required

| Trigger |
|---|
| The customer is **high ML/TF risk** |
| An individual is a **foreign politically exposed person** |
| A person is **located in, or was formed in, a high-risk jurisdiction** |
| You must submit an **SMR** in relation to the customer **and intend to continue** providing them with a designated service |
| The designated service is part of a **nested service relationship** |
| The service would involve **unusual patterns of transactions**, transactions that are **unusually large or complex**, or that have **no apparent economic or legal purpose** |

### 11.2 What it involves

- Collecting and verifying **more** KYC information
- Collecting and verifying information on **source of funds** and **source of wealth**
- Taking additional measures to understand the background, ownership and financial situation of the customer **and other parties to the transaction**
- More in-depth monitoring and analysis of transactions and behaviours
- **Increasing the frequency of relationship reviews**
- Updating KYC information more frequently

### 11.3 Worked example

An 18-year-old wants to engage a conveyancing firm to buy a high-value property using physical currency, to expand their "property portfolio". During CDD they list their occupation as "student".

That triggers enhanced CDD. The firm collects and verifies **source of cash and source of wealth**. The customer says it came from an inheritance and provides a **grant of probate**.

**Note what resolves it:** an independently verifiable document that explains the wealth. Not an assertion.

### 11.4 Politically exposed persons

Three types:

| Type | Definition |
|---|---|
| **Domestic PEP** | Holds a prominent public position or role in an Australian government body |
| **Foreign PEP** | Holds a prominent public position or role with a government body in another country. **Includes foreign PEPs working or residing in Australia** |
| **International organisation PEP** | Holds a prominent public position in an international organisation such as the UN, WTO or NATO |

**Only a foreign PEP automatically triggers enhanced CDD.** Domestic and international organisation PEPs are risk-assessed.

## 12. Customer risk ratings

You must have a **simple, documented method** for rating clients, appropriate to the size and nature of your practice - typically low, medium and high.

**Key rules:**

- The rating is driven by key client attributes and transactions.
- **A rating is not fixed.** It must be reviewed and updated when circumstances change: new services, unusual activity, or changes in ownership or control.
- **Higher-risk clients get greater scrutiny**; lower-risk clients can be managed with standard controls.
- **Record why a client received a particular rating**, so you can demonstrate your decision-making to an independent reviewer or to AUSTRAC.

**Features of an effective system:**

- Methodology consistent with the wider risk assessment
- Clear and logical
- Considers relevant risks and typologies, and both the sector risk assessment and the national risk assessment
- Aligned with previous risk incidents within the practice
- Provides for **judgemental overlays** to be applied and recorded
- **Fit for purpose** - neither overly complex nor overly simplified
- Produces accurate ratings

## 13. Reliance on another entity's CDD

**What it is.** One reporting entity may rely on CDD completed by another, provided the legislative requirements are met. It can operate case by case or under an ongoing arrangement.

**The rule that matters: responsibility cannot be outsourced.** The relying entity remains legally responsible for ensuring the CDD is compliant.

**Before relying**, satisfy yourself that the other entity has appropriate AML/CTF controls and **can promptly produce the underlying CDD records** if required.

**The practical recommendation: do your own CDD** unless there is a clear commercial benefit, genuine confidence in the other entity's framework, and an established working relationship. This matters especially now, while everyone's compliance frameworks are still maturing.

**If you do enter a reliance agreement**, it should at minimum define each party's responsibilities, the scope of reliance, and the process for sharing CDD records.

## 14. Transaction monitoring

### 14.1 The link to CDD

CDD tells you **who your customer is**, including their risk score. Nature and purpose tells you **what is normal and expected** for them. Monitoring detects **divergence from that baseline**.

Without a properly documented nature and purpose, monitoring has nothing to measure against.

### 14.2 What a suspicious transaction looks like

Hard to define precisely, but as a general rule a suspicious transaction or activity is one that is **inconsistent with the customer's known legitimate business or personal activities, or with what is normal for that type of customer**.

### 14.3 Narrations

Record against every transaction alert:

- Details of the **nature and purpose** of the transaction
- Information **collected from the customer** about it
- An **overview of the investigation** performed
- Reference to any relevant **supporting documentation**
- An explanation of **why the transaction was or was not** considered unusual or suspicious, and the actions taken
- Where a suspicion is formed: **when and why** it was formed, and reference to the reporting undertaken

**Anything relating to an SMR must be held on a strict need-to-know basis.** See the tipping off section.

**The narration is the evidence that you had a system.** A cleared alert with no narration looks identical to an alert nobody looked at.

## 15. Red flags for lawyers

Memorise these. They are the operational output of everything above.

**Structure and anonymity**

- Customer seeks to use an **agent, nominee or intermediary without obvious reason**, especially cross-jurisdictionally
- Customer **seeks anonymity**
- Customer is a **company or trust with complicated beneficial ownership**
- **Creation and use of complex legal person arrangements for no apparent reason**, especially cross-jurisdictionally
- **Creation of complicated structures with no apparent economic reason**

**Money movement**

- Customer uses a **professional business or trust account**, particularly where large cash deposits are made
- **Funds received from a foreign jurisdiction** where there is no connection between that jurisdiction and the customer
- **Overseas instructions with no economic reason**

**Client profile**

- Customer **is not concerned about the level of fees**
- Customer appears to have **access to cash substantially above their means**
- Customer uses a **virtual office**
- Customer **invoices and trade accounts inconsistent with known business activity**

**Transaction profile**

- Customer has a **fast-growing real estate portfolio**
- **Purchase amount unusual compared with appraised value or the previous purchase amount**

**A red flag is not a stop sign.** It is a signal to pause and ask further questions or conduct enhanced CDD. Often further questions will satisfy you. Sometimes they will not, and then you report.

## 16. Reporting to AUSTRAC

### 16.1 The three reports

| Report | Trigger | Timeframe |
|---|---|---|
| **Suspicious Matter Report (SMR)** | Suspicion on reasonable grounds that a person is not who they claim to be, or that a matter is linked to criminal activity or proceeds of crime. Covers suspected money laundering, fraud, terrorism financing, sanctions breaches and unusual transactions | **24 hours** where the suspicion relates to **terrorism financing**; **3 business days** otherwise; **5 business days** where the SMR includes information protected by legal professional privilege |
| **Threshold Transaction Report (TTR)** | Any cash or cash-equivalent payment of **AUD $10,000 or more**, **regardless of suspicion** | **10 business days** |
| **Annual compliance report** | Summarises how you met your obligations for the reporting period | Expected **31 March** annually. **Confirm the period and date with AUSTRAC** - published guidance has been inconsistent |

### 16.2 The point of SMRs

Worth knowing because the reporting can feel pointless. In New Zealand, where the equivalent regime has run longer, roughly **17,000 suspicious activity reports** are produced annually, of which about **300 come from lawyers and accountants**. Of those 300, **over 100 are used to commence police investigations** - because reporting from professional services is so detailed and well-evidenced.

Banks produce the highest volume. Professional services produce the highest quality.

Both AUSTRAC and its NZ equivalent are under-resourced, so in practice the register is used **reactively**: investigators searching for known names rather than proactively working every report. Your report may sit until it is needed, and then be the thing that connects a syndicate.

### 16.3 Continuing to act after an SMR

If you submit an SMR and intend to **continue providing a designated service** to that customer, **enhanced CDD is triggered**.

Whether to terminate the retainer is a separate and much greyer question. There is little guidance. Document your reasoning either way.

---

## 17. Tipping off

**Tipping off is a criminal offence** under the AML/CTF Act, carrying a maximum of **2 years imprisonment or 120 penalty units, or both**.

In substance: you must not disclose to the customer, or to anyone else, information that would or could reasonably be expected to prejudice an investigation.

**The practical consequences:**

- SMR information is held on a **need-to-know basis only**.
- Your file notes and narrations must not be visible to staff who do not need them.
- **You cannot explain to the client why you are asking additional questions** in terms that reveal a suspicion.
- You cannot tell the client you have reported them.

**This is the provision that most directly conflicts with the ordinary solicitor-client relationship**, and it is the source of most practitioners' discomfort with the regime.

---

## 18. Legal professional privilege

### 18.1 The protection

AUSTRAC's guidance is that the AML/CTF Act **does not affect the right of a person to refuse to give information where that information would be privileged** on the grounds of legal professional privilege. AUSTRAC will not require a reporting entity to submit information subject to LPP.

### 18.2 The mechanics

- If some of the information you are required to give is subject to LPP and you withhold it on that basis, **you must submit an LPP form** in lieu of the withheld information.
- **You do not need to submit an LPP form if all of the information constituting the grounds of suspicion in an SMR is subject to LPP.**
- Where an SMR includes LPP-protected information, the reporting timeframe extends to **5 business days**.

### 18.3 The conduct rules

[ASCR r 9.2.2](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.9) permits a solicitor to disclose information confidential to a client where the solicitor is **compelled by law** to disclose it. That is the exception that accommodates AML reporting.

Two conduct rules were amended in consequence of the reforms: **[r 8](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.8)** (accepting and following instructions) and **[r 13](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.13)**, which now expressly includes as just cause for withdrawal "any circumstance where a client has not provided all information requested of them that is required to fulfil a solicitor's statutory obligations".

### 18.4 Where to draw the line

The international guidance is to **report everything unless it is clearly privileged**; where you are in the grey zone, regulators would rather have more information.

That does not sit comfortably with a solicitor's instincts, and it should not be applied mechanically. Use the Law Society's guidance and, where a claim is genuinely arguable, take it seriously - but understand that the regulator's default position is disclosure.

**Resources:** the Law Society of NSW's *Protecting Your Clients' Claim to Client Legal Privilege*, and the Law Council of Australia's *Guidance Note on client legal privilege and federal regulators*.

### 18.5 Front-end the problem

**The single most useful practical step.** At the very start of any client conversation about a matter, set out what your statutory obligations are should they disclose certain things.

You cannot warn them after a suspicion has formed. You can tell every client at the outset what the rules are. Build it into your engagement letter and your first meeting script.

---

## 19. Record keeping

**Make and keep records for at least 7 years.**

That applies across CDD records, verification documents, risk assessments and ratings with reasons, transaction monitoring narrations, reports submitted, training records, and the program documents themselves.

**Privacy overlay.** Legal practices providing designated services must comply with the *Privacy Act 1988* (Cth) when handling personal information for AML/CTF purposes - collection, use and storage for CDD, monitoring and record keeping. The OAIC has published guidance for reporting entities. Collecting identity documents for AML creates a data holding you are now responsible for securing.

## 20. Training and employee due diligence

### 20.1 Who must be trained

Effectively **everyone who touches any component of AML**. Not just the compliance officer:

- **Fee earners** providing designated services
- **The trust account manager**, because they handle client funds
- **Administrative staff** who collect identification
- **Summer clerks** working in a conveyancing unit

The only genuinely ancillary exceptions are staff with **no client interaction at all**, such as a marketing team producing collateral and events. Even they may drift into the grey zone.

### 20.2 What the program must ensure

- Appropriate staff are trained in the applicable aspects of the legislation and are aware of your policies, procedures and processes
- Training is **tailored to the person's specific responsibilities**
- The compliance officer receives **additional, periodic training**
- The governing body and senior management stay informed of changes in the Act and Rules, your regime, and your wider risk profile

### 20.3 Suggested frequency

| Group | Frequency |
|---|---|
| AML/CTF compliance officer and senior management | Every **6 to 12 months** |
| Customer-facing personnel | Every **12 months** |
| Personnel responsible for onboarding, transaction monitoring or enhanced CDD | Every **12 months** |
| All other personnel not in AML/CTF roles | **At onboarding** (general awareness) |

### 20.4 Employee due diligence

AUSTRAC's non-exhaustive list:

- Identify and verify identity
- Confirm **employment history and qualifications**
- Undertake **character and background checks**
- Confirm the person is a member of the relevant professional association and **is not, and has not been, subject to disciplinary action**
- Decide whether they are suitable and assess whether they pose a risk to the business
- Undertake a **National Police Check**

**High-risk roles** may require additional checks, including **PEP screening** and **credit or bankruptcy screening**.

---

## 21. Reporting to senior management

What the compliance officer should be reporting regularly:

- **Customer risk rating distribution and movements**
- **Overdue ongoing CDD reviews**
- **SMR escalation and filing trends**
- **Transaction monitoring alert volumes and outcomes**
- **Repeat assurance findings or recurring issues**
- **Staff AML training completion and competency testing**
- **Regulatory updates and emerging typologies**
- **Independent audit progress and remediation status**
- **Quality assurance testing outcomes**
- **Resourcing and capability concerns** within the AML function
- **Program changes or methodology updates**
- **Significant media or enforcement actions** relevant to the sector

In a small practice this is a short standing agenda item, not a report pack. **But it must be minuted**, because the governing body's oversight obligation is tested by evidence.

---

## 22. Independent evaluation

- Required in certain circumstances and **at least once every 3 years**.
- The reviewer must be genuinely **independent** - no prior involvement in the program.
- The evaluation must assess whether the program **adequately identifies and addresses** your ML/TF risks, and whether it is **being effectively implemented in practice**.
- Findings must be **documented and reported** to the governing body or senior management, and **remediation actions tracked**.

**First-evaluation deadlines are staggered** under the transitional rules, and the staggering is derived from digits in your AUSTRAC enrolment number. It is easy to get wrong. **Work out your date, write it down, and diarise it.**

**Book early.** There is a genuine shortage of AML professionals in Australia and reviewers are being booked years ahead.

## 23. Consequences of non-compliance

| Consequence | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Regulatory enforcement action** | Formal action to address breaches and require correction of controls |
| **Civil penalties** | Imposed by the Federal Court. Up to **100,000 penalty units** for the most serious contraventions |
| **Mandatory remediation and reviews** | Remediate deficiencies, engage independent reviewers, undertake external audits |
| **Formal directions and notices** | Infringement notices, remedial directions, enforceable undertakings. May be made public |
| **Loss of registration or ability to operate** | Where the entity poses an unacceptable ML/TF risk |
| **Professional discipline** | Law societies have indicated they would regard an AML compliance breach as a matter of interest as a **breach of professional standards** |
| **Reputational damage** | Enforcement outcomes may be publicly disclosed |

### 23.1 Remediation is the underrated risk

Remediation sounds mild because it is usually private. It is extremely expensive.

Real examples: a remedial instruction requiring termination of every client who could not be brought across the CDD line within an unreasonable deadline, which forced one business to cancel **68% of its book overnight**. A bank hiring a team of six to work long hours at high rates remediating a loan book that had since ceased to exist.

### 23.2 The penalty record

| Entity | Penalty | Cause |
|---|---|---|
| Westpac (2020) | **$1.3 billion** | Over 23 million breaches, including failure to report international transactions |
| Commonwealth Bank (2018) | **$700 million** | Over 53,000 breaches relating to cash deposit machines |
| Crown Melbourne and Crown Perth (2023) | **$450 million** | Systemic failures |
| SkyCity Adelaide (2024) | **$67 million** | Serious program breaches |
| Tabcorp (2017) | **$45 million** | Multiple failures and lack of proper reporting |
| 16 small businesses (2024) | Various | Failure to submit annual compliance reports |

**Two things to take from this list.**

First, **Westpac was a systems failure, not a decision to conceal.** Legislation came in, certain transaction types had to be reported, the coding was not set up correctly, and it could not be fixed in time. The scale of the penalty reflected the fact that some of the unreported transactions were possibly linked to child exploitation payments. **High harm drives the penalty.**

Second, **small businesses are fined too**, and the commonest reason is the most mundane: failing to submit the annual compliance report.

### 23.3 The personal dimension

Enforcement against individuals is career-ending even where the individual was not laundering anything. The directors pursued in the casino matters were not laundering money; they failed in their AML roles. For a professional who has spent a career reaching partner level, that outcome is worse than most alternatives.

## 24. How AML interacts with the rest of your practice

### 24.1 Trust accounting

- Your trust account is one of the three vulnerabilities AUSTRAC identifies. Funds leaving a law firm trust account are frequently **not scrutinised** by receiving institutions.
- **Cash of $10,000 or more triggers a TTR**, regardless of suspicion. The trust accounting rule is unchanged: cash goes into the general trust account first ([LPUL s 143](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/act-2014-16a#sec.143)).
- The old *Financial Transaction Reports Act 1988* pathway is gone. Do not use old precedents or checklists referring to Form 15A.
- Record **nature and purpose** at matter level including **use of the trust account** and any **third-party payer**.
- See the **Trust Accounting** guide.

### 24.2 Conduct rules

- **[ASCR r 9.2.2](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.9)** - compelled by law is an exception to confidentiality.
- **[ASCR r 8](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.8)** - only accept and follow instructions that are lawful, proper and competent.
- **[ASCR r 13.4](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.13)** - failure to provide information required for your statutory obligations is now just cause to withdraw.
- See the **Practice Management** guide, ethics section.

### 24.3 Risk management

Client onboarding, file opening and matter acceptance all now carry an AML gate. Integrate it into existing intake rather than bolting it on. See the **Risk Management** guide.

## 25. Implementation checklist

**Immediate**

- [ ] Confirm which designated services your practice provides, using AUSTRAC's Professional Services Core Guidance and the "check if you'll be regulated" tool
- [ ] Confirm you are enrolled for **all** of them
- [ ] Confirm your AML/CTF compliance officer is formally appointed and notified to AUSTRAC
- [ ] Confirm the risk assessment exists, is documented, and justifies its conclusion
- [ ] Confirm the AML/CTF program exists and has **senior management approval** recorded

**Systems**

- [ ] Document the customer risk rating methodology and record reasons for each rating
- [ ] Build nature and purpose capture into matter opening, covering trust account use, expected volume, parties and third-party payers
- [ ] Build a CDD checklist for individuals, companies and trusts, including the 25% beneficial ownership and effective control tests
- [ ] Build an enhanced CDD trigger list and procedure
- [ ] Build a transaction monitoring narration template
- [ ] Set up need-to-know handling for SMR material so tipping off cannot occur inadvertently
- [ ] Diarise your **independent evaluation** date, worked out from your enrolment number
- [ ] Diarise the annual compliance report

**People**

- [ ] Map everyone who touches AML: fee earners, trust account staff, administrative staff taking ID, clerks
- [ ] Set the training calendar: 6 to 12 months for the compliance officer and senior management, 12 months for everyone client-facing
- [ ] Implement employee due diligence for new hires, including a police check and confirmation of no disciplinary history
- [ ] Set a standing AML agenda item for partner meetings and **minute it**

**Client-facing**

- [ ] Front-end your statutory obligations in the engagement letter and first meeting
- [ ] Brief the team on the red flag list
- [ ] Decide and document your position on reliance agreements
- [ ] Review pre-1 July 2026 clients for ongoing CDD and monitoring
