# Risk Management - Practical Guide

Professional indemnity, professional negligence claims, supervision, and practice risk. NSW focus. Stated as at 8 September 2026.

---

## 1. The headline numbers

If you remember nothing else from this guide, remember these four figures. They tell you exactly where to put your attention.

| | |
|---|---|
| **37%** | of all claims arise from **property and conveyancing** |
| **42%** | of all claims are caused by **poor communication with clients** |
| **23%** | are caused by **not knowing the law adequately** |
| **>90%** | of cyber claims are caused by **business email compromise** |

Claims are overwhelmingly **not** about black letter law. They are about the contributing factors: time pressure, underpricing, poor file notes, undefined retainers, weak supervision, and failures of basic interpersonal skill.

The policy is designed to be **broad based**, not to carry clever exclusions. Lawcover's claims solicitors are practitioners who have been in private practice, and the question they ask on notification is how the policy **can** respond, not how to avoid it.

### 2.4 What is covered

- **Any form of civil liability** arising from the provision of legal services
- **Claims for compensation**
- **Personal costs orders**

### 2.5 What is not covered

- **Dishonesty and fraud.** The policy covers mistakes. Where there is a fraudulent person inside the practice, **the other people in the practice remain covered**.
- **Fee disputes**
- **Your own commercial interests outside practice.** If you join a client's board, your own D&O and other insurances are your responsibility. Think about conflicts too.
- **Disciplinary investigations.** The policy does not respond to defence costs for a disciplinary complaint.

### 2.6 Are employees always covered? *Zakka v Elias*

**Not always.** *Zakka v Elias* [2013] NSWCA 119 is the cautionary case and it should be briefed to every employed solicitor.

A graduate solicitor with about six months' experience was asked by a family member to advise a friend on loan documentation for a business refinance - two loans totalling around $700,000. She declined for a while, then agreed to do it **in her own time and free of charge**. She sent some correspondence from the firm's email system. The partner knew nothing about it.

The friend later claimed against the firm alleging negligent advice causing loss. Lawcover's policy could respond for the principal, **but not for the employee**. The Court found this was:

> a clear case of a solicitor engaging in a frolic of her own or at her own whim. It is an act Ms Rahe was not authorised to take as an employed solicitor of the firm. There was not a sufficient connection between that unauthorised conduct and Ms Rahe's employment to bring this within the scope of the doctrine of vicarious liability.

Some of her advice was found technically incorrect. She faced potential **personal liability of $700,000** in the early part of her career.

**The practical fix.** Have an explicit conversation with every employed solicitor about work for family and friends:

- Whether the firm takes on that client is **at the principal's discretion**.
- What fees are charged is **at the principal's discretion** - normal rates, an increased rate if the client is high risk, or a discount.
- Nothing is done outside the firm's file and system.

### 2.7 Civil claims against solicitors

| Cause of action | |
|---|---|
| **Torts** (negligence) | |
| **Breach of contract** | |
| **Consumer law** | |
| **Disciplinary complaints** | |

**The elements of a negligence claim.** The client must prove breach of duty of care or breach of retainer, plus **causation** and **damage**. An error in a document that causes no financial loss will not get a claim up. The standard of care is that of a **reasonably prudent practitioner**, varying according to expertise.

**Duties can also be owed to non-clients**: beneficiaries under a will, beneficiaries under a trust, and others who rely on the advice. Wills and trusts are where this bites most.

### 2.8 Mistakes happen

Worth saying plainly: **it is acceptable to make mistakes.** Every lawyer does. The best lawyer in the country can be sued for negligence. It is part of being a professional, and there is insurance support for it.

In most legal claims the loss is **dollars, and dollars can be replaced by dollars**. That is not true of, say, a medical negligence case where the patient may carry a permanent impairment.

**What matters is how you respond.** The destructive responses are well documented:

- **Anger** - the urge to fire off an abusive letter to the client. Do not send it. Draft it, then send the draft to a trusted colleague for feedback.
- **Denial** - head in the sand. Lawcover has received notifications days before a court hearing, which makes a reasonable defence almost impossible to mount.
- **Loss of confidence** - triple-checking every document and number, which is torturous and unsustainable.
- **Loss of interest in practice.**

**Notify early.** The policy is designed to help you.

### 2.9 Suing a client for fees

You have every right to. Think hard first.

**What you hope will happen:** the client breaks into a sweat, rushes to the bank, transfers the funds and emails an apology.

**What actually happens:** retaliation. A complaint to the Office of the Legal Services Commissioner alleging overpricing, lack of professionalism and delay, plus a cross-claim alleging negligence thrown in for good measure. You then respond to a vexatious claim at your own cost in time and money. Practitioners routinely say afterwards that they wish they had not sued.

**AI has made this materially worse.** Disgruntled clients now generate voluminous complaints through AI platforms and send them to multiple regulators. The volume alone is punishing to respond to.

**Better options, in order:**

1. **Have a conversation.** Meet face to face if the fee warrants it. A cold call to the client's office, sitting in reception, has worked.
2. **Get your file costs assessed.** It is timely and costly, but it is excellent insurance in itself: it confirms the file is in order and the fees are appropriate, and it substantially reduces the chance of a successful claim or a complaint with merit.
3. **Get money on account up front.** The best prevention.

## 3. Where claims come from

### 3.1 By area of law

| Area | Share of claims |
|---|---|
| **Conveyancing** | **37%** |
| **Litigation** | 25% |
| General commercial | 10% |
| Family law | 10% |
| Wills and estates | 10% |
| Mortgages and commercial borrowing | 5% |
| Leases | 2% |
| Sale and purchase of business | 1% |

**Wills and estates, general commercial, and property are the three biggest areas of practice work in NSW by volume.** Combined, wills and estates plus general commercial produce 20% of claims. Conveyancing alone produces 37%.

Conveyancing has historically run in the low 20s, spiked to 43%, and has settled at 37%. It has been the standout for too long.

### 3.2 Why conveyancing

Three compounding causes:

**1. Legislative change.** The dominant recent driver traces to the 2016 introduction of NSW foreign purchaser surcharge duty and foreign owner land tax surcharge. The legislation was rushed, confusing and convoluted, and the NSW definition of a foreign purchaser differed from the Commonwealth's. Practitioners now face questions of 200-day residency, visa status, and whether there is a foreign purchaser **within a trust**. Revenue NSW has audited aggressively, found shortfalls, and issued notices to purchasers for duty plus interest plus penalties. Purchasers pass those on as claims against the firm. **The volume is extremely high and it is not going away.**

**2. Time pressure.** Conveyancing is a high-volume, time-poor area, and the fundamentals get missed - including basics such as tenants in common versus joint tenants.

**3. Pricing.** This is the underrated cause. **Conveyancing today is a very different beast from thirty years ago** in terms of the obligations on the solicitor, yet the fees charged are often eye-wateringly small for the work that should be done. When fees are too low because of perceived competitive pressure and client expectation, firms cut corners, the solicitor is less involved in the transaction, knowledge is not kept current, and claims follow.

**The practical conclusion: there is no point banging your head against the wall in an area that is barely profitable or unprofitable.** If your conveyancing pricing is right, well done. If it is not, fix it or exit.

### 3.3 Litigation

High by the nature of the work. One party is usually disgruntled with the outcome and typically takes action against their professional adviser. In emotionally charged work such as family law, **both** parties may.

### 3.4 Generalist practice

Not separately quantified, but a recognised high-risk profile. Firms are encouraged to **focus on their skill sets rather than trying to be all-encompassing**. Regional isolation is a real constraint, but no longer an absolute one - counsel and specialists are available online, and a small regional practice does not have to do criminal, workers' compensation and personal injury simply because it is the only firm in town.

## 4. The causes of claims

### 4.1 The breakdown

| Cause | Share |
|---|---|
| **Poor communication with clients** | **42%** |
| **Not knowing the law adequately** | **23%** |
| **Document problems** | **16%** |
| **Systems problems** | **16%** |
| Other (including no negligence) | 3% |

"Other" includes claims where there was **no negligence** - the client simply having a go. Those can still be costly, and may have to be settled where there is no documentation in place.

### 4.2 Poor communication - 42%

A lack of common understanding between solicitor and client: the solicitor does not appreciate the client's expectations, or the client does not listen to or understand the advice.

| Sub-cause | Share |
|---|---|
| Failure to advise | 15% |
| Client instructions not followed | 10% |
| Incomplete explanation or advice | 9% |
| Failure to define extent of retainer | 4% |
| Failure to obtain instructions | 4% |

**The uncomfortable observation.** In the cold light of day, when relaxed, with time to listen, ask questions and explain in different ways, most practitioners are good communicators. The 42% is what happens **under pressure**.

**And it is the one thing nobody measures.** Firms measure file opening, file closing and file reviews well. Almost no firm drills into communication skills - between solicitor and client, within teams, or with the other side. That is the gap.

### 4.3 Not knowing the law adequately - 23%

| Sub-cause | Share |
|---|---|
| Lack of legal knowledge | 14% |
| Incorrect advice | 9% |

This is normally in the low to mid teens. It has been elevated by the conveyancing surcharge duty and land tax issues.

**The dominant sub-cause is practising outside your area of expertise**, and it is usually driven by money or fear, not ignorance:

- Fear of losing the client to a competitor if you refer the matter out.
- Billing pressure to take the work to get money in.

**This happens in large firms too.** One claim involved an accredited specialist in business law who did a will for a client. Substantial drafting errors made it very expensive. The principal acknowledged two causes: he was behind on billings that month, and he did not want to pass the client to another team within his own firm for fear of losing them. Internal adversarial structures create claims.

### 4.4 Document problems - 16%

| Sub-cause | Share |
|---|---|
| Failure to check documentation | 9% |
| Drafting error | 7% |

Errors that leave the practice unchecked. Usually caused by time pressure imposed by the client, or by simple volume.

### 4.5 Systems problems - 16%

| Sub-cause | Share |
|---|---|
| Lack of file maintenance | 6% |
| Oversight | 4% |
| **Cyber** | **3%** |
| Delay or alleged delay | 2% |
| **Lack of supervision of staff** | **1%** |

**The 1% for supervision is significantly under-reported.** It appears in the data only where a principal has the insight to say "I was not supervising adequately", which is rare. More often the account given is of a hopeless employee who made mistakes repeatedly. Sometimes that is genuinely an unfortunate recruit. But where it happens repeatedly, the real questions are about training, induction, meetings, and the quality of supervision between the supervising solicitor and the employee. **Gaps in those areas usually indicate poor leadership overall.**

**Delay at 2%** understates the problem too. Where someone is stuck on a matter, that is the indicator that a system is needed, and that the culture must allow someone to put their hand up and say so.

## 5. The single most important message

**Have it in writing.**

- The letter to the client
- **File notes, file notes, file notes**

**A good file note can extinguish an allegation.** Without documentation, a claim becomes very difficult to defend. Judges expect a solicitor in private practice to have evidence of the advice they gave.

### 5.1 File notes - the practical rules

- **There is no time limit on making a file note.** If it is September and you realise you never noted a July meeting, **make the note tonight**.
- **Date it today.** If handwriting it, put today's date and describe the earlier meeting or call. Electronic notes are better because the system time-stamps them.
- **Never backdate a file note.** Practitioners have done it under pressure or distress and turned an omission into a **fraudulent document**.
- A note made today about a July 2026 conversation is far better than nothing when the claim arrives in 2032.

### 5.2 The discipline that gets abandoned

The pattern that produces the worst exposure: a client of twenty years, the families have become friends, matters have been handled the same way for a decade. Then there is a perceived financial loss and the client sues.

At that point the retainer is not detailed, the costs agreement is thin, and the file notes are not thorough. **The file is poor because the relationship was built on trust.**

**The discipline of a professional is to maintain the same standard of file structure for every matter, regardless of the client.** For a difficult or high-risk client, **more** than the standard: more detailed file notes, more meetings, documented phone calls, everything recorded.

**This applies to discounted and pro bono work.** "I was charging a family member rate" is not a defence. The quality of the work, and of the file, must be identical. **You do not discount the file notes.**

## 6. Clients who bite

| Client type | The risk |
|---|---|
| **The disappearing client** | Fails to respond |
| **The "don't involve me" client** | Wants the solicitor to make the decisions |
| **The transferred client** | Won't pay the former solicitor's fees; the file is usually poor |
| **The extended family client** | Who is actually the client? |
| **The experienced claimant client** | Notorious fee dodger |
| **The non-English speaking client** | Who is interpreting? |
| **The family and friend client** | Potential for conflict and lack of detailed file notes |

### 6.1 The disappearing client

Ask yourself: **how would our attempts to reach this client present under cross-examination?** "We sent a letter" is probably insufficient. Document each attempt: call, no answer, voicemail left, support staff attended, follow-up letter.

### 6.2 The "don't involve me" client

They may genuinely believe that is what they are paying for - drop the problem off like a car at a mechanic and collect it when it is fixed. Or they may think it mitigates their own risk. Family and friends do this most. **Keep them engaged so they truly understand, and document it.**

### 6.3 The transferred client

Ask practitioners to rate the last transferred file they received out of ten. The answer is usually **two to five**.

**Do not refuse transferred matters. Do run due diligence, and tell the client what you are doing:**

- Conflict check
- Review the file to confirm you have **sufficient resources and people available**
- Confirm you have the **whole file** - there may be a lien over it with the previous firm
- Assess the quality of the file
- Explain that you must **review the work done in its entirety**. You cannot pick up at the letter L and rely on the previous firm for A through K
- Ask about objectives and expectations afresh
- **Ask whether they saw anyone before that firm.** If the answer is yes, you are the third firm, and you should think hard

One matter joined **six firms** as defendants. It began with a sole practitioner, was transferred on as it grew more complex, and ended with one of the largest firms in Australia. The problem throughout was **assumptions made along the way**.

### 6.4 The extended family client

Everyone arrives to discuss mum or dad's will. **Clear out the non-clients.** Remind yourself who the client actually is, and have the confidential conversation with them alone. What the family thinks the parent wants and what the parent actually wants are frequently different.

### 6.5 The non-English speaking client

**Use an independent interpreter**, even if you or a staff member speaks the language. There is judicial authority emphasising independence.

### 6.6 The disruptive client

A distinct category worth naming. Not the innovator or entrepreneur, who is to be encouraged, but the **disruptor**:

- Drip-feeds information
- Drops large volumes of documents and calls shortly afterwards expecting advice
- Has done their own AI research and believes they know more than you
- Shifts goalposts
- Tells support staff something different to what they told the partner
- **Disrupts the work you are doing for other clients**

**On the AI-armed client**, there is a commercial opportunity in unbundling. Offer the choice: we work from your material, which means reading all of it and charging for that, or we work from the firm's templates.

### 6.7 What clients feel

Many clients find dealing with a solicitor very uncomfortable. They may feel: **suspicious, worried, sceptical, insecure, threatened, at risk, impatient, angry, exposed.**

None of that is visible in an email thread. **Periodically pick up the phone** with regular clients to understand what pressures they are under.

## 7. Dealing with the client

Professional negligence claims arise from failures in basic interpersonal skills - failures to **listen, ask and explain**:

- **Not enough time devoted to the initial interview.** If the client arrives late, do not compress an hour's meeting into 25 minutes. Corners get cut.
- **Failure to probe deeply enough**
- **Failure to explain advice effectively**
- **Too quick to find a solution** - latching onto what looks like the legal issue and failing to advise of all the options
- **Not enough time devoted to routine advice**
- **Failing to define the retainer**
- **Failing to confirm advice in writing** - both letter to client and file note

### 7.1 The expectation gap

There is usually a very large gap between what the client believes legal work involves and what it is. **Probe for it up front.** Ask what their expectations and objectives are. Bring the unrealistic back towards reality.

Then explain **the firm's expectations of the client**. Remind them it is **their** matter and they have responsibilities if it is to progress efficiently.

### 7.2 Explaining to different clients

Do you explain advice the same way every time, regardless of the client? Consider the kind of intelligence you are dealing with:

- **Academic or conceptual** - comfortable with a written advice
- **Financial** - an accountant may read an organisation through numbers beautifully but struggle with twelve pages of legal advice
- **Visual or design** - far better served by diagrams and workflow charts
- **Street smart or emotional** - do you relate to them, or is it always stop-start?

### 7.3 Communicating value

A major and widespread gap. Lawyers describe **what the work is and what the process involves** very well. They rarely describe **the value of the work to the client**.

This is most acute in conveyancing. If the value is never explained, whatever fee you charge will look like a lot.

## 8. Supervision

### 8.1 The rule

> **37.1** A solicitor with designated responsibility for a matter must exercise reasonable supervision over solicitors and all other employees engaged in the provision of the legal services for that matter.

[ASCR r 37](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.37)

### 8.2 Why supervision matters

| | |
|---|---|
| **Compliance** | ASCR r 37 |
| **Manages risk** | The claims driver behind a great deal of the 16% systems category |
| **Staff development and satisfaction** | |
| **Leverage** | You cannot leverage people you do not supervise |
| **Client satisfaction and retention** | |

### 8.3 What "reasonable supervision" means

*Legal Services Commissioner v Michael Vincent Baker* [2005] LPT 002 at [42]:

> A practitioner should properly supervise all legal professional work carried out on their behalf. Vicarious liability aside, a practitioner's legal and fiduciary duties to a client are not avoided or reduced by delivering that client into the care of an employee, whether or not that employee is legally qualified. The supervision required however varies according to the employee's experience, qualifications and role and with the type and complexity of the work.

**It depends** - on experience, qualifications, role, and the type and complexity of the work. Over time the supervisee should take more involvement with less supervision. But the structure must be deliberate.

**Reasonable supervision is much more than an open-door policy.** It requires appropriate systems, clear communications, and support and structure.

### 8.4 What poor supervision produces

**Legal Services Commissioner v Dingwall [2017] QCAT 76** - no supervision or feedback, no file reviews, no clear supervisor, no formal risk management plan in place.

**Victorian Legal Services Commissioner v Ibidapo Olayemi [2019] VCAT 1283** - a visa application matter; the wrong category chosen from a drop-down list; the visa refused; a bungled cover-up; the client lied to. Poor supervision and a practitioner struggling with workload.

**McLennan v Clapham and Ors [2019] ACTSC 1** - no supervision of an experienced conveyancer, who did not appreciate what legal advice should have been given. **The law firm breached its duty of care to the clients.**

A related pattern seen in practice: a firm delegating conveyancing to a non-lawyer clerk under a structure where the contract was handed to the client to read, with an invitation to call if there were issues. The clerk did nothing wrong. But asbestos issues in the property materially affected the purchase price, the purchasers claimed, and **the firm was criticised by the judge over its internal structures**. The question the structure must answer is: **who is doing the legal work?**

### 8.5 Distress as a cause of misconduct

Two cases worth holding in mind, because they are about workload rather than character.

**A solicitor with seven years' experience** was asked by a client for an update and told them the matter was at a point it was not. She lied. Immediately after the call she went to her supervising partner and admitted it. She was referred for a disciplinary hearing. A former partner gave evidence in her defence about the firm's leadership and management structure: she had been absorbing the files of other solicitors going on leave and maternity leave, with an ever-increasing load.

**A graduate solicitor**, a partner's first recruit, was fast and accurate on visa applications. The workload increased, and so did the demand for speed and quality, to the point where he **collapsed at work from exhaustion**, which the partner did not notice. He clicked the wrong drop-down box, the visa was refused, and he pasted together a scanned document telling the client it had been approved. It was so obviously wrong that the client immediately phoned the firm. He admitted it at once. The partner said he would have no hesitation employing him again.

**The lesson.** Under prolonged pressure and distress, judgment and decision making falter, and people make decisions they never would in a clear state of health. When reviewing dishonesty in disciplinary matters, ask what circumstances were operating.

**And build a culture where a mistake can be raised.** Where someone says "I made a mistake" or "I am stuck on this file", the response must not berate them, or the next person will stay silent.

### 8.6 Supervision structure

**Identify the right person**

- Know the requirements of the task
- Recognise staff strengths and weaknesses
- **Monitor individual workloads**

**Explain and assign roles**

- Explain the task, in detail
- Agree on who is doing what
- Set clear expectations and time frames

**Develop rapport and a relationship**

- Share knowledge, skills and experience
- Encourage a problem-solving attitude
- Help staff plan and prepare for challenges
- **Check in with staff to support wellbeing**

**Make time**

- Block out designated times
- **Stick to scheduled meetings**
- Plan ahead and organise your workload

Repeatedly setting and cancelling meetings with a junior is demeaning. It tells them they are not important and destroys the value of the meeting. If you are a litigator and often out of the office, schedule times you can actually commit to.

**Carry out regular file reviews**

- Have a file review schedule
- Flag and discuss issues; provide constructive feedback
- Document and follow up actions

**Bring the junior solicitor into the file review** even where they have had minimal involvement, so they learn the whole matter. File reviews also surface the fundamentals: have all monies been received, has the file been closed, has the client been written to confirming the matter has concluded.

**Use checklists**

- Client identification
- Conflict of interest
- Matter workflow
- File closing

### 8.7 Delegation

Asked whether they are good at delegating, around **98% of principals say no**. That is good insight. The follow-up question is whether they are getting any training in how to delegate better.

The failure mode is familiar: "I do not have time to explain it, I will do it myself, it will be faster and more accurate." Do that consistently and **you should not have staff**, because you are excluding them from the work and from their own development.

### 8.8 Practice tips for the supervised

Worth circulating to junior staff:

- **Don't wing it and don't assume - if you don't know, ask**
- Ensure all client documents and communications, including emails, are signed off or checked before being sent
- Use **shared calendars for critical dates** and reminder systems
- Develop the habit of **contemporaneous, comprehensive file notes** of all client interaction. This builds a complete picture of the file and provides documentary evidence of interaction, instructions received, advice provided and work undertaken
- Make sure you have **regular uninterrupted time with your supervisor** to discuss work in progress and any concerns
- **Ask for feedback**

### 8.9 Remote supervision

Additional risk, requiring a bigger effort in online engagement. Also consider:

- **Paper files at home.** How are they secured? At the end of a matter, are documents going into the household recycling bin, or coming back for proper shredding or secure disposal?
- **Shared accommodation.** What are the security arrangements? You do not want family or housemates glancing at screens.
- **The standard in the home environment should match the standard in the office.**

### 8.10 A red flag: staff turnover

High turnover of staff is a high-risk indicator. There may be legitimate explanations - a team leaving for a competitor. But in smaller firms it often indicates a **culture of churn**, and it says something about the quality of leadership.

### 8.11 Promoting the wrong people

Partners are often promoted because they are excellent rainmakers who bring in revenue and engage clients well. **That is not the same as being a good people manager.** If they are not, question why they are being put anywhere near people management. It is a high-risk area.

**Do leaders in your firm get any training in leadership, management and supervision?** If not, that is where to start.

## 9. Business management as a risk

Claims arise less from black letter law than from weak foundations. A technically excellent practitioner with poor peripheral management is a claims risk. Mid-tier firms that had existed for over a hundred years have failed for this reason.

### 9.1 Common characteristics of claims-prone practices

Drawn from corporate failure analysis and from Lawcover's practice visits:

- **Hubris and overconfidence.** Believing your own opinions rather than drawing on external views that may challenge your assessment of performance.
- **Working in silos.** This happens in a two-partner firm as readily as in a corporation: both partners effectively running their own practices, with very little cross-discussion about financials, policies, procedures, or how different teams operate.
- **The buck stopping at the top** in a culture where it cannot be challenged.
- **No vision, not documented, not reviewed.**

### 9.2 The strategic plan

Document and review annually, ideally away from the office:

| | Description |
|---|---|
| **Vision** | What the organisation wishes to be like in some years' time. Well conceived, it defines the aspirations of where a business wants to be, is simple and memorable, and is an overarching statement closely tied to values and goals |
| **Mission** | The steps to reach the goal. How it will be done. This may be to maintain the status quo |
| **Values** | Articulated as a list of principles. Describes the desired culture. A behavioural compass for all staff. Published values also say something to clients |
| **Goals** | Relates to strategy and vision. Where the firm wants to be, what it is aiming to achieve |

**A vision exercise worth doing now, not later.** When will you leave practice? What does that look like? Visualise it. Review it each year; it may change. **If you are in a partnership, keep those discussions going** so you are not blindsided by a key partner announcing a three-month exit with no succession plan in place.

### 9.3 SWOT

Not a consultant's ritual. The feedback from firms that do vision planning, SWOT and business planning is that it is effective.

**What Lawcover sees without it is drift**: a gradual deterioration in performance over time, small decreases rather than one systemic failure.

Run a SWOT on the practice, and separately on your **key clients**. Also test the basics:

- Where is the practice located, and **what is the business rationale**? Convenience to home is a legitimate answer for a sole practice - but what is the marketplace like around it?
- **Have you benchmarked the fees charged by other firms in your area** for the services you offer?

Look at what is coming down the pipeline so you are not blindsided. Build a table feeding into the business plan:

| 2. Practice areas | | | |
| **3. Technology / AI** | | | |
| **4. Cyber crime risk management** | | | |
| **5. Regulatory reforms** | | | |
| 6. Recruitment | | | |

### 9.5 The business plan

**How do you plan?**

- Legal services **offered**
- Legal services **not offered**
- **The ideal client** - characteristics, visualise, lock it in

**Back yourself as a sole practitioner.** Document in policy what you do and what you do not do. Be willing to say "we cannot help you" and refer out. **Keep to your practice areas.**

**The ideal client, from the workshop:**

- Pays, and **pays on time**
- **Honest**
- Gives **clear instructions**
- **Responsive** - delivers information in a timely manner as the matter requires
- **Listens**
- Shows **respect**
- **Refers** other like-minded clients
- Brings **repeat work**

**Write it down and lock it in.** It is far easier to target ideal clients once you have defined them. The un-ideal client is the opposite of each.

**Financial plan**

- Budget
- Profitability
- Cash flow
- **WIP, debtor days, invoicing**
- Pricing policy
- **Discounting - to be validated**
- Retained capital

**Do not price by seniority.** "I am an eight-year lawyer therefore my fees are here" may have worked in the 1970s. Work from what the firm needs for profitability and retained capital, then check that against the market. If your required fees are far above the market, the answer may be a different market or a different area of work altogether.

**Measure:**

- **WIP** - at least measure time
- **Debtor days** - 30, 60, 90 should be front of mind. **90-plus is unacceptable** unless there is a business rationale, such as a no-win-no-fee personal injury practice
- Ask why, repeatedly, and do not let it blow out

**If you are not good at invoicing, get someone in who is. If you do not like calling clients about outstanding fees, get someone in who does.**

- Budget
- Forms of marketing
- Client relationship development
- Testing and validating

**IT plan**

- Budget

- **Data security**
- **Cyber plan - cyber risks, response and support**

**People plan**

- Budget

### 10.3 The double excess - read this twice

The PII schedule provides:

> **Excess:** EXCEPT THAT **excess** means **twice that amount** for **claims** arising from any payment or electronic funds transfer made on an instruction or authorisation that the **law practice did not take reasonable steps to verify**.

**If you pay out on an unverified instruction, your excess doubles.**

That single clause is the commercial case for a verification procedure. Build it, document it, and make it non-optional:

- **Independently verify account details before making any payment.** Banks now display the payee account name before confirmation - use it.
- Set a value threshold above which you **telephone the payee on a number you already hold**, never one taken from the email.
- Require **secondary verification** of any change to payment instructions.
- Use **dual authorisation** for EFTs.

### 10.4 The response plan

| Action | Steps |
|---|---|
| **Risk based assessment** | Undertake a thorough assessment of your practice information security requirements. Take steps to make information and cyber security part of your normal business risk management procedures. Train and educate staff in cyber security principles so they become part of your practice culture |
| **Asset audit** | Your practice is only secure if everything is protected. Audit any assets potentially at risk - identify financial, personal and other information assets that are critical, and the IT services you rely on |
| **Vulnerability assessment** | Assess your cyber security resilience and identify where you may have vulnerabilities. Assess all the IT equipment within your practice, including mobile and personal IT devices. Understand the technical and organisational risks and how they are currently managed |
| **Expert advice** | Decide whether you need expert advice to undertake risk and vulnerability assessments and to get the right protection and security controls in place, regardless of whether your IT is outsourced or in-house |
| **Risk framework and governance** | Put technical and practice measures in place to satisfy the security obligations relating to personal data and to control the risk of cybercrime. Monitor their effectiveness on an ongoing basis |
| **Accountability** | **Appoint a senior member of staff to oversee data and cyber security.** Ensure they have the right resources and support |
| **Cyber security policies** | Prepare and issue clear policies and procedures on all key aspects of data and cyber security. All staff should be made aware of their obligations. Include policies on staff use of business internet facilities, use of social media, and policies on bring your own device |
| **Monitoring and review** | Review systems and procedures regularly and respond to changes or problems, including attacks or disruption. Monitor and improve security controls to keep pace with your IT equipment, services and information. **Dispose of programs or physical devices properly** - remove software or equipment you no longer need, ensuring it contains no sensitive information. **Manage user access**: create accounts when staff join and deactivate them when they leave |
| **Cyber incident management** | **Have a Cyber Incident Response Plan.** If your practice is disrupted or attacked, this plan guides your response, ensures adequate measures are taken to contain the threat, and enables quick recovery where protection controls did not prevent the incident |
| **Record keeping** | Keep records, including details and evidence of your risk-based assessments, the technical and organisational measures taken to protect the security of personal and client data, your processes for testing and evaluating their effectiveness, and cyber-incident management |

### 10.5 Questions to ask your IT provider

A ready-made due diligence list. Use it when appointing or reviewing a provider.

**Governance and resilience**

1. **Security certifications** - does your company hold any relevant security certifications (for example ISO 27001, SOC 2)?
2. **Incident response plan** - what is your incident response plan in case of a cyberattack? How often is it tested and updated?
3. **Staff education and training** - how do you train your staff on cyber security best practices? Are there regular training and education sessions they can attend? Can you facilitate a schedule of **phishing simulations and testing**?
4. **Third-party risk management** - how do you assess and manage the security risks posed by third-party vendors and suppliers?
5. **Data backup and recovery** - what is your backup and recovery strategy? How often are backups performed, and how are they stored and protected?
6. **Business continuity plan** - do you have one in place to minimise disruption in the event of a cyberattack?

**Technical controls**

7. **Network security** - what measures protect the network from unauthorised access and cyberattacks (firewalls, intrusion detection, intrusion prevention)?
8. **Endpoint security** - how do you protect endpoints (computers, laptops, mobile devices) from malware and other threats?
9. **Email security** - what measures protect against email threats such as phishing and spam?
10. **Logging** - do you have SIEM or SOAR for detecting and alerting on anomalous issues?
11. **Data encryption** - do you encrypt sensitive data both **at rest and in transit**?
12. **Access controls** - how do you manage user access to systems and data? Are there strong password policies and **multi-factor authentication** in place?
13. **Vulnerability management** - how do you identify and address vulnerabilities in systems and software?
14. **Penetration testing** - do you conduct regular penetration testing, and how do you respond to findings?

**Compliance and communication**

15. **Data privacy compliance** - how do you comply with the Privacy Act, Health Records Act, Consumer Law and other relevant regulation?
16. **Incident reporting** - what is your policy for reporting data breaches and security incidents to relevant authorities?
17. **Insurance coverage** - do you have adequate cyber insurance to protect against financial losses?
18. **Proactive monitoring** - how do you proactively monitor our network and systems for signs of malicious activity?
19. **Threat intelligence** - how do you stay informed about the latest threats and vulnerabilities?
20. **Communication and transparency** - how will you communicate with us in the event of a security breach?
21. **Experience and expertise** - what is your experience in providing cyber security services to **law practices**?

### 12.3 The checklist

**Insurance**

- [ ] Confirm your primary and any top-up cover, and whether run-off is arranged for the top-up

- [ ] Read the excess clause in your PII schedule and confirm you have a documented payment verification procedure

**File discipline**

- [ ] Define the retainer in writing on every matter, including limited retainers
- [ ] Confirm all advice in writing to the client and in a file note
- [ ] Make file notes contemporaneously; never backdate; date today's note today
- [ ] Apply the same file standard to discounted, family and pro bono work

**Clients**

- [ ] Document your ideal client characteristics and use them
- [ ] Build a transferred-file due diligence procedure
- [ ] Use independent interpreters
- [ ] Clear non-clients from the room and have the confidential conversation with the client
- [ ] Get money on account

**Supervision**

- [ ] Set a file review schedule and keep to it
- [ ] Schedule regular uninterrupted supervision time and **do not cancel it**
- [ ] Monitor individual workloads
- [ ] Get leadership, management and supervision training for anyone supervising
- [ ] Build client identification, conflict, workflow and file closing checklists
- [ ] Address remote working security: paper files, disposal, shared accommodation

**Business**

- [ ] Document vision, mission, values and goals; review annually, off site
- [ ] Run a SWOT on the practice and on key clients
- [ ] Complete the horizon gazing table and feed it into the business plan
- [ ] Benchmark your fees against the local market
- [ ] Review conveyancing pricing specifically against the actual obligations
- [ ] Track WIP and debtor days; investigate anything past 90 days

- [ ] Appoint a senior person accountable for data and cyber security
- [ ] Write the Cyber Incident Response Plan
