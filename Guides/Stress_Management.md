# Stress Management and Wellbeing - Practical Guide

For legal practitioners. Includes the self-assessment activity. Stated as at 8 September 2026.

---

## 1. Why this one matters

**The Australian legal profession has one of the highest rates of depression of any profession - roughly four times the rate of other professions.**

| | |
|---|---|
| **1 in 3** | solicitors affected by depression and anxiety |
| **85%** | of legal professionals have experienced anxiety |
| **>60%** | have experienced depression or knew someone in the workplace who had |
| **>30%** | of legal professionals report experiencing burnout |
| **4x** | more likely than other professions to experience high rates of depression and anxiety |

**And across the population generally:**

| | |
|---|---|
| **1 in 5** | adults experienced a mental health concern in the past year |
| **Almost half** | the Australian population will experience mental illness in their lifetime |
| **65%** | of those people **will not access treatment** |
| **~1 in 4** | employees take time off each year due to feeling mentally unwell |
| **$290 billion** | cost to the Australian economy in 2024 |
| **<7%** | of government health revenue goes to mental health services, despite mental illness being the nation's **second largest health burden after cancer** |
| **+30%** | increase in psychological workers compensation claims last year, against +11% for physical claims |

Psychological claims are more costly, take longer to identify, and last longer.

**The 65% figure is the operative one.** Two thirds of people experiencing a mental health condition will not seek help themselves. That makes recognising it in others, and having the conversation, a practical skill rather than an optional kindness.

**This guide takes a harm-minimisation approach.** The goal is not to tell you to change your working hours or take up running. It is to help you understand what stress is doing, recognise it, and apply techniques that work.

## 2. What drives it in legal practice

Raised in the session by practitioners themselves:

**Structural**

- **Heavy workloads and long hours.** Lawyers consistently work longer hours than comparable professional clients.
- **The lumpy workload.** Work dumped at the last minute with an artificial sense of urgency, over which an employee has no control.
- **Limited control over workload leads to helplessness.** Low job control is the single most reliable predictor.
- Multiple stakeholders, all of whom consider their matter urgent.
- Lack of role clarity.
- Poor or absent communication.

**The nature of the work**

- **High stakes.** Real consequences for real people.
- **Difficult and demanding clients.**
- **Ethical and moral dilemmas.** Including, specifically, **moral distress from inside the firm** - being asked to do the wrong thing and having to decide whether to stand up to it.
- **Vicarious trauma.** Clients share distressing accounts and that trauma transfers to you.

**Culture**

- Toxic leadership.
- **Bullying from the bench.**
- **Partners who have forgotten what it is like to be a junior** - "this is how I was treated, now it is my turn". This is reported frequently.
- Imposter syndrome and the automatic negative voice.

**Who the profession attracts**

Type A, highly intelligent, high expectations of self and others. And specifically **the two Ps: perfectionism and pessimism.**

Both traits make you better at the job. Being discerning, ensuring everything is 100% accurate, anticipating what will go wrong - these are professional virtues. **They also both correlate with lower wellbeing.** That is the tension at the centre of this, and it does not resolve neatly.

**The downstream effects**

Significant stress produces poor physical health: high blood pressure, sleep problems, digestive problems. And **higher rates of alcohol and substance use** driven by the emotional strain of the work.

## 3. What is actually happening

### 3.1 The physiology

As soon as your brain registers a **threat** - and the threshold differs for everyone - you go into an automatic fight-or-flight response.

| System | Role |
|---|---|
| **Limbic system** (hippocampus, hypothalamus, amygdala) | Mobilises the body to respond to stress. Fight, flight or freeze. **Bypasses executive functioning.** Reactionary and spontaneous |
| **Prefrontal cortex** | Decision-making, problem-solving, judgement and moral reasoning. Rational, analytical, logical, data-based |

**The limbic response bypasses the prefrontal cortex** - the part you need for good decisions on behalf of clients.

That part of the brain has not evolved. It is still preparing you to fight or run: blood diverted to major muscle groups, elevated heart rate, elevated breathing, cold extremities, nausea from diversion away from the digestive system, and **attentional narrowing** - useful in the short term, but it means you stop noticing other relevant cues.

**It takes conscious thought to come back down.**

### 3.2 Why modern stress becomes chronic

Historically, having escaped the threat, you would sit down and return to a parasympathetic state.

**Today you do not fight or run, and you do not sit under the tree.** You deal with the thing and move straight to the next one. That is how chronic stress builds.

**The cortisol connection.** Sustained cortisol depresses the immune system. That is why you get sick on the first weekend of a holiday - your body has been holding it together and finally stops.

**The implication.** You need **both** ongoing practices that maintain wellbeing **and** in-the-moment techniques to bring the nervous system down when the difficult call comes in.

## 4. Two techniques that work in the moment

These are the most immediately usable things in this guide.

### 4.1 The vagus nerve technique - 40 seconds

The vagus nerve is the largest nerve in the body. It starts in the brain, branches down the spinal cord, and ends in the groin area. **Activating it flips you from sympathetic dominant (limbic) to parasympathetic dominant (cortex accessible).**

**Method one - the lower end.** Think of the three body structures where the nerve terminates: pelvic floor, buttocks, sphincter.

- **Squeeze for 4 seconds. Relax for 4 seconds.**
- **Repeat for five cycles - about 40 seconds.**

**Method two - the upper end.** Gently press on the back of your closed eyelids.

- **Press gently for 4 seconds. Relax for 4 seconds.**
- **Repeat for 40 seconds.**

**This is evidence-based**, with substantial research behind it including work by Dr Eric Gentry on post-traumatic stress patients. It sounds too simple. It works.

**Where practitioners actually use it:** one client uses it when a judge is shouting at her in court. Another uses it when she wakes at 3am ruminating and getting progressively more wound up.

Note that many people already do the eyelid version instinctively - throwing off their glasses and pressing. **Sometimes the body knows what to do before you do.**

### 4.2 Box breathing - also called combat breathing

Used by the navy. You are already breathing, but under stress you breathe from the **thoracic region** rather than the diaphragm, which reinforces the stress response.

**Box breathing:**

- **Breathe in for 4**
- **Hold for 4**
- **Breathe out for 4**
- **Hold for 4**

Picture a box.

**Extended out-breath** is the other technique: breathe in for three counts, **breathe out for six**. Doubling the exhale is what does the work.

**This is regarded as one of the most powerful ways to change your physiology deliberately.**

## 5. The mental health continuum

Mental health is not binary. You are somewhere on a continuum, and it moves day to day depending on everything going on in your life.

| **IN CRISIS** | **STRUGGLING** | **SURVIVING** | **THRIVING** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Severe ongoing distress | More intense and ongoing distress | Mild and temporary distress | Optimal functioning |
| Difficulty functioning well | Loss of control | Feeling irritable, anxious or down | Adaptive growth |
| Hopelessness | Do not feel like normal self | Muscle tension | Wellbeing |
| May include mental health conditions, burnout, insomnia, substance abuse | Negative thinking | Less focused | Motivated |
| | Feeling overwhelmed | | |

**Check in with yourself daily: where am I today, and what do I need?**

**And remember what else is loaded on top.** Work is not the only input. There is the juggle outside work, parenting, elderly parents and the sandwich generation. There is cost of living and fuel prices. And there are geopolitical events - one executive coaching client, asked how he had gone with his coaching homework, replied: *my country is at war*.

**The point of intervention is "struggling".** If nothing is done at that stage, the continuum runs towards crisis, where a diagnosis may follow.

## 6. Recognising the signs

Signs may be **physical, cognitive, emotional or behavioural**. Everybody's presentation is different, which is why the self-assessment at section 7 matters.

**Physical.** Frequent headaches or migraines. Sleep problems. Digestive upsets. Body tension, typically neck, shoulders and back. Catching colds and flu more readily - because cortisol depresses the immune system.

**Cognitive.** Mental disorganisation. Forgetting things you know. One reported example: standing next to his wife while introducing her to a new client and being unable to recall her name. Negative thinking becomes exacerbated, and you become more susceptible to cognitive biases.

**Emotional.** Black-and-white thinking. Inability to see both sides of an issue. Anxiety, sadness, loneliness, cynicism. Focusing only on problems.

**Behavioural.** Acting on the emotions - and **these are the ones other people see**. Being argumentative or angry. Or withdrawing and going quiet. Diminished libido. Withdrawing from people.

**The WHO definition is worth holding onto.** Mental health is the ability to manage stress, live and learn, and contribute to your community. **Stress is a normal part of life.** The question is not whether you experience it but how it is manifesting in you.

### 7.5 Why start with the GP

Your GP can rule out other medical causes first. If nothing else explains it, they can administer a **DASS** (Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale). If that is elevated, they can write a **mental health care plan** and refer you to a psychologist, which gives you **up to 10 Medicare-rebated psychology sessions**.

Psychology may also be covered by private health insurance.

### 7.6 If you scored highly

**Treat it as a red flag and act on it.**

The pattern practitioners present with, and the reason this matters, is cumulative. Someone arrives having dealt with this their whole career. They have done very well professionally because they were so committed. **The family has gone. No friends, no hobbies, no life. And they are physically unwell.** Then they ask what they should do.

**Help is available, and the evidence is clear that it is effective.** The value is in acting years earlier than that.

## 8. Self-awareness

The foundation, and the reason for the survey.

- **Know your triggers.** Not just symptoms - what sets them off. A worked domestic example: two small children wanting to try several outfits each morning while you are trying to leave for work. The children need autonomy, which is a core psychological need. The solution was not to eliminate the choice but to **narrow it to three options**, which removed the friction without removing the need.
- **Pay attention to signs and symptoms.** If you carry tension in your back and shoulders, **get a massage**. It is one of the most effective ways of removing the physiological effects of stress, and it is affordable and available. A tennis ball against a wall works too.
- **Choose positive coping strategies.**
- **Notice the negative, self-critical voice.** It sometimes drives motivation. Far more often it is unhelpful. **Self-criticism aggravates anxiety, which puts you into the limbic state.** Self-compassion is not a nicety: being kind to yourself, acknowledging what went wrong and treating it as learning, keeps the cortex engaged. Kristin Neff's work is the reference point.
- **Minimise screens and social media.** Screens emit blue light which suppresses melatonin production, the chemical you need for sleep. Use night modes, and aim for at least an hour - ideally longer - screen-free before bed. On social media, note that the rise in youth anxiety tracks closely with the arrival of front-facing phone cameras.
- **Watch what you consume.** The media knows about the negativity bias, and bad news sells. Good news stories are rare. Your brain remembers what you feed it. **Observe what you are focusing on and choose.**
- **Observe others and choose your response.**

### 8.1 Alcohol

Worth stating plainly and without preaching. The Australian guidelines: **no more than 10 standard drinks per week and no more than 4 in one sitting**, with **one or two alcohol-free days per week**.

**The mechanism that matters here.** Your liver processes alcohol around 2am, which is when you wake. The effect of alcohol on sleep is substantial, and poor sleep compounds everything else in this guide.

## 9. PERMA - the essential elements of wellbeing

Martin Seligman's framework, from the founder of positive psychology, who has taken a specific interest in lawyers.

| | Element | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | **Positive emotion** | Finding joy, gratitude |
| **E** | **Engagement** | Flow, deep focus in work |
| **R** | **Relationships** | Support networks |
| **M** | **Meaning** | Purpose, values-driven work |
| **A** | **Accomplishment** | Achievement, mastery |

### 9.1 Positive emotion

**Know what brings you joy.** If you take one thing from this guide, take that.

It is a diagnostic as much as a practice. Clients who are least well often cannot name a single thing.

Barbara Fredrickson's research shows that **positive affect makes you more cognitively flexible**: more innovative, more creative, better able to see connections between things. That is also why being in nature works - it makes you feel good, and you think better.

Practise **gratitude** deliberately, to counterbalance the negativity bias.

### 9.2 Engagement

**Flow.** Csikszentmihalyi's concept: the state where you are so engaged that time disappears and you do your best work.

**Flow occurs when challenge and skill match.** Too much challenge produces anxiety; too little produces boredom. **Create the conditions** - make sure the skill level matches the challenge level in the work you take and the work you allocate.

### 9.3 Relationships

**Relatedness is one of three core psychological needs.** Introvert or extrovert, you need connection. This is why COVID was so damaging for many.

**Sole practitioners: this is your structural risk.** You are on your own, and connection has to be deliberately built rather than arriving with the job.

### 9.4 Meaning

**Reconnect with why you came to law in the first place.** It is easy to be consumed by billable hours, clients and the win-loss game and to lose sight of the purpose.

### 9.5 Accomplishment

Mastery and achievement - the third core psychological need, **competence**.

**The negativity bias sabotages this.** At the end of the day you focus on what went wrong. Deliberately note what you accomplished: clients helped, problems solved, juniors mentored.

### 9.6 The self-determination link

The three core psychological needs are **autonomy, relatedness and competence**, and all three are available through work. That is professional wellbeing. It produces higher motivation, better performance and job satisfaction. **Its absence produces dissatisfaction, disengagement and increased burnout risk.**

Having good mental health is more than the absence of illness:

| Domain | Why a lawyer needs it |
|---|---|
| **Mental wellbeing** | To think clearly, make good decisions, proactively manage stress |
| **Physical wellbeing** | Energy levels, concentration, overall health |
| **Emotional wellbeing** | Ability to understand, manage and express emotions - essential in high-conflict work where **your clients are also under stress** |
| **Professional wellbeing** | Sense of purpose and satisfaction |

## 11. Five Ways to Wellbeing

The Black Dog Institute's evidence-based framework. Adoptable at any point in life.

1. **Connect**
2. **Be active**
3. **Keep learning** - and not just law. Learning a recipe, a dance, how to braid your daughter's hair. Novel skill acquisition is the point.
4. **Take notice** - awareness and mindfulness
5. **Give** - helping others, including small acts of kindness

## 12. Wellbeing practices

The full list from the session:

- Prioritise health
- Exercise and relaxation
- Sleep hygiene
- Time management
- Manage client demands
- Boundary setting
- Mindfulness
- Meditation
- Cognitive reframing
- **Recognise what you can and cannot control**
- Growth mindsets
- Restorative environments
- **Get support**

### 12.1 Exercise

**You get one body, look after it.**

**The most controllable part of your day is the morning.** Get up slightly earlier and get exercise in before the day takes over. Or walk at lunch.

If you do not enjoy exercise, build in **incidental** activity - park further from the station, take the stairs. The objective is elevating your heart rate, not enjoying the process.

### 12.2 Sleep hygiene

- **Turn off screens** well before bed
- Low light; music if it helps
- **Do not eat or drink too close to bedtime**
- **Get up and go to bed at the same time every day**
- **Have a fixed pre-bed sequence and always do it in the same order** - kiss the children, bathroom, teeth, bed. The order is what trains the response

### 12.3 Time management

Simple and effective: at the start of the day, work out what has to be done, in what order, and when each thing will be fitted in. Then tick things off - which also feeds the **accomplishment** element of PERMA.

### 12.4 Boundaries with clients

Difficult when you are highly accessible, but achievable by framing it as service quality: **tell clients when you will be most effective in supporting them.**

At home, physical boundaries matter too - closing the office door is a real intervention, not a token one.

## 13. Mindfulness

> "Between stimulus and response, there is a space. In that space lies our freedom and power to choose our response. In our response lies our growth and freedom."
>
> Viktor Frankl

### 13.1 What it actually is

**Paying attention, on purpose, in the moment, without judgement.**

You build the mindful muscle through meditation practice. If you are sceptical - and many are - note that Google, Microsoft and similar organisations dedicate entire floors to it for their employees. **You get one body, look after it. You get one mind, look after it.**

### 13.2 Practices

1. **Box breathing**
2. **Mindful pause**
3. **Grounding and anchoring techniques**
4. **Body scan**
5. **Walking meditation**
6. **Guided meditations**
7. **Mindful eating**

### 13.3 It is simpler than it sounds

**Mindfulness is paying attention to your five senses.** That is the whole mechanism. Let the thoughts go and come back to the body and the senses.

It can be swimming - following the black line, aware only of breathing and sensation. It can be running. It can be folding clothes or drinking a glass of water.

One practitioner's end-of-week ritual: pick up the glass and feel it, drop in the ice and watch it move, listen to it, open the bottle and smell it, pour and listen to the chink, bring it to the lips and taste. **Five senses, one activity.**

**Choose one thing you do every day and do it mindfully.**

### 13.4 The doorknob technique

The single most practical application here.

You come home carrying everything from the day - the difficult confrontation, the thing that went wrong. You walk in, the children are running around, and you say "yeah, yeah" - **but you are not there**.

**As you put your hand on the doorknob, make a deliberate choice: I am now going to be mindful.** Leave those thoughts outside. Open the door and be present.

That brings you joy and it builds relationships. **When you are thinking, you are not necessarily being effective.**

## 14. Cognitive reframing

The core premise of cognitive behavioural therapy, heavily abbreviated:

**The event does not cause the consequence. Your thoughts about the event cause the consequence.**

Someone cuts in front of you in traffic. It is not the event that produces the stress - it is the thought. **Change the thought and you change the consequence.**

### 14.1 Working with unhelpful thoughts

- **Our thoughts are real, but they are not necessarily true.**
- The automatic negative voice - called the *homunculus* in the psychology literature - sits on your shoulder narrating.
- **Cognitive defusion**: place the thought over there and look at it, rather than holding it as fact.

### 14.2 Do not suppress

**What we resist will persist.**

Think of a balloon pushed underwater. The moment you relax, it surfaces. Past traumas and emotions sit there until you tease them out and face them.

**Walk towards the tension.** It is unpleasant and counterintuitive - we prefer equilibrium and the path of least resistance - **but the path of least resistance is not the path that stops the suffering**.

### 14.3 Unravel the thoughts

Your brain under stress is a ball of wool. **We unravel our thoughts through our lips and our fingertips.**

That is why speaking to a psychologist or a trusted colleague works, and why **writing things down** works. Both externalise the tangle so you can see it.

## 15. Workplace culture and the employer's duty

### 15.1 What good culture requires

- **Leadership: role modelling behaviour, offering support**
- Peer support programs
- Collaboration, inclusion and trust
- Reward and recognition
- **Role clarity**
- Transparent communication, feedback and psychological safety

### 15.2 The legal obligation

**The employer is responsible for managing psychosocial hazards in the workplace.** This is a positive statutory duty, not a policy aspiration.

Under the [*Work Health and Safety Act 2011* (NSW)](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/act-2011-010), a person conducting a business or undertaking must ensure, so far as is reasonably practicable, the health and safety of workers.

Since **1 October 2022**, the [*Work Health and Safety Regulation 2017* (NSW)](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2017-0404) has contained an express duty. Division 11 of Chapter 3, Part 3.2 provides:

- **cl 55A** - a **psychosocial hazard** is a hazard that arises from or relates to the design or management of work, a work environment, plant at a workplace, or **workplace interactions or behaviours**, and may cause psychological harm.
- **cl 55B** - a **psychosocial risk** is a risk to health or safety arising from a psychosocial hazard.
- **cl 55C** - a PCBU **must manage psychosocial risks** in accordance with the Regulation's risk management provisions.

*Note: the course slide referred to a "Work Health and Safety Act 2023". There is no such Act in NSW. The operative instruments are the 2011 Act and the 2017 Regulation as amended in 2022.*

**Psychosocial hazards identified in the SafeWork NSW Code of Practice** map almost exactly onto section 2 of this guide:

- Job demands
- **Low job control**
- Poor support
- **Lack of role clarity**
- Poor organisational change management
- Inadequate reward and recognition
- **Traumatic events or material**
- Remote work
- Violence and aggression
- Bullying and harassment

**The practical consequence for a principal.** The things described in this guide as cultural problems - the lumpy workload, the absence of control, the partner who treats juniors as they were treated - are **regulated hazards** you have a statutory duty to identify, assess and control. Identify them, apply control measures, and review whether the controls work. See also the supervision material in the **Risk Management** guide, where two disciplinary cases turned on workload and exhaustion rather than character.
