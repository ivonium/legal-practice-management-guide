# Practice Management - Practical Guide

Covers the Day 1 material: introduction to practice and financial management, the session notes toolkit (fixed fee pricing, cost of production, office manual, managing partner role), business planning, ethics, and business development and marketing.

## 1. Introduction to Practice and Financial Management

### 1.1 The market you are entering

The working assumptions to plan against:

- **The "death of lawyers" is overstated.** The profession will thrive. But commoditisation of legal services is accelerating.
- **AI is a tool for lawyers, not a competitor.** The point of difference is in funding it and in knowing how to instruct it. It is not reducing lawyer workload so much as raising the standard expected.
- **Work from home is here to stay in some form.** The profession is too atomistic to force change, and flexibility has become a career definer for younger lawyers.
- **Financial success is a function of structure, not of location or size.** A well-structured regional sole practice can out-earn a badly structured city firm.
- **The cost/price squeeze is intensifying.** Firms have moved from price setters to price takers.
- **The profession is feminising.** In 2011 the NSW profession was 54% male; by 2024 it was 44% male. Among practitioners under 25 the split is roughly 73% female, and among those over 75 it is roughly 87% male. The strategic question is who changes to suit whom.

### 1.2 What a winning firm looks like

Four characteristics:

**Appropriate structure for each practice group.** Do not apply the same performance criteria to different groups. A commoditised conveyancing team and a complex disputes team are different businesses inside one firm.

**Get the people puzzle right.**
- Hold people accountable for **what they can control**. This is the difference between "free range" and "battery hen" management (see 1.7).
- Be comfortable with different career paths. Not everyone is heading for equity.
- Build compensation systems that focus on the wider firm, not just individual billings.

**Pricing.**
- Charge more than your cost of production. Always.
- Charge clients the way they want to be charged.

**Have a point of difference.** What makes your firm unique in the market that others cannot or will not emulate? If that fails, **be likeable**.

### 1.3 The profit driver equation

This single equation applies to Australia's largest firm and its smallest with equal force:

> **Net profit per principal = (1 + L) x BR x CH x R x M**

| Driver | What it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **L - Leverage** | Employed fee earners per equity principal | The "1 +" captures the principal's own fee-earning capacity |
| **BR - Billing rate** | Weighted average charge rate across all fee earners | Not the partner rate. The **weighted average** |
| **CH - Chargeable hours** | Weighted average annual chargeable hours per fee earner | 1,100 hours is roughly 5 per day |
| **R - Realisation** | Conversion of WIP into billings | Most firms write off about 15%, so R = 85% |
| **M - Profit margin** | What is left after salaries and overheads | Around 30% before principal salaries, give or take 10% |

**Worked example - an established firm.** Leverage 2.5 (so 3.5), weighted average rate $350, 1,100 hours, 85% realisation, 35% margin. Result: roughly **$401,000 net profit per principal**.

**Worked example - a part-time sole practice.** No employees (so 1.0), $500 per hour, 5.5 hours a day, 3 days a week, 44 working weeks (726 hours), 90% realisation, 70% margin. Result: roughly **$229,000**. The law is a design-your-own-adventure business.

### 1.4 Two things the equation tells you

**First: being consistently average produces a good result.** The $401,000 above came from being average on every driver. The difficulty is being consistently average. No managing partner ever motivated a team by asking them to be as average as possible, but it works.

**Second: net profit per principal is not take-home pay.** It has to cover: a salary for your role as a lawyer; a return on the capital you invested; retained earnings or debt repayment to fund the practice's cash flow; and tax. What you can actually spend is likely to be 50% to 70% of the figure.

### 1.5 Where to intervene

| Driver | Constraint | Usable? |
|---|---|---|
| **Leverage** | Market: you must find the people **and** have the work for them | Biggest impact, hardest to move |
| **Price** | Market: it must be acceptable to clients | Biggest impact, most used lever |
| **Chargeable hours** | Internal | Housekeeping. Fix it tomorrow |
| **Realisation** | Internal | Housekeeping. Fix it tomorrow |
| **Margin** | Internal | Housekeeping, but a **secondary** driver |

**The performance bands:**

| Net profit per principal | What it takes |
|---|---|
| ~$350,000 | Average on all five drivers |
| $350,000 to $700,000 | **Very good** on two, one of which must be leverage **or** price (you will not get both), plus average on the other three |
| $700,000+ | Very good on two, average on the rest, **plus a homogeneous culture** - specifically a culture that does not tolerate aberrant behaviour |

Build the equation in a spreadsheet so you can test scenarios. If you are starting out, model yourself plus nobody and see what price and hours you actually need.

### 1.6 Leverage

**The trend:**

- Headcount leverage in mid-tier firms has been broadly flat, around 3.2 to 3.7 employed fee earners per principal over fifteen years.
- **Support ratios are falling sharply**, from around 0.78 to around 0.53 support staff per fee earner. Established small firms are now around 0.2.
- High leverage in commodity work is shifting to technology and outsourcing.
- **Leverage via lawyers will reduce.** Work is flowing to senior people. Clients increasingly refuse to pay for juniors to learn on their matter, which strands mid-level lawyers.
- Support levels track the age of practitioners. Older practitioners have higher support needs.

**What to do with this:**

- **Staff for the trough, not the peak.** Most firms staff a standing army ready for work that may not come. Better practice in a tight market is to staff at the low point and buy capacity when demand spikes. For a new practice, your first hire should probably be a **contractor**, not a full-time employee. Buy an hour to sell an hour. Outsourcing does not have to mean offshore.
- **Be conscious of legacy thinking.** Support roles persist because "we have always done it this way". No support person is going to volunteer that their role has been automated away. Test what each back-office function actually produces.
- **Be accepting of legacy partners.** Long-tenured partners near the end of their careers will not change. Sometimes the correct strategy is to wait them out rather than fight.
- **High leverage is often a compensating factor for low productivity.** Firms with uniformly poor productivity tend to carry high leverage because it is easier than having a difficult conversation. In a stagnating market that becomes a financial anchor.
- **Leverage swings wildly when the denominator changes.** Five fee earners over two principals gives leverage of 2.5. Promote one fee earner to principal and it becomes four over three, or 1.33. This is precisely why promotion to equity in small firms is hard: it adds a mouth to a trough that has not grown.

### 1.7 Productivity and chargeable hours

**The trend is clearly down.** Mid-tier firms last averaged six chargeable hours a day in 1999. They now average about 5.2. Recorded hours and effective hours are both trending down, for partners, associates and solicitors alike. Meanwhile expectations have gone up, producing a widening delivery/expectation gap.

**Why, when everyone reports being busy?** Three causes:

**1. We measure things lawyers cannot control, so they game the system.** A lawyer who has consumed 80% of an estimate having done 40% of the work faces a choice: have an uncomfortable conversation with the client about a revised estimate, or stop recording time. They understand the firm's tolerance for write-downs, and they manage to it. Since they are judged on write-downs they do not control, they stop recording. That is where the missing hours go.

**2. There are no consequences.** In most firms, missing budget produces a pay rise and a new budget. There is safety in numbers: as long as someone is worse than you and nobody has been moved on, nothing happens.

**3. Price has compensated.** Firms have held profit steady by raising rates, so the pain point never arrives. Arguably the price increases drove the productivity decline. Management responds with anxiety rather than action.

**The two ways to manage lawyers:**

| Battery hen | Free range |
|---|---|
| Here is your salary. In return produce $X in fees. Here is your hourly rate, so here are your required hours. We will also measure your write-downs, your debtors and your WIP. We have expensive software, so we will look at it weekly and yell at you monthly | Delighted to have you. I will give you as much work as you can chew through. Do it to the best of your ability and **capture the activity**. Record your hours. If it is fixed fee work, recording hours matters more, because we need to know the cost of delivery |

**Free range people for as long as you can, then battery hen them.** You cannot go back the other way. Free range is where the lawyer still believes in Santa; battery hen is where they know there is no Santa.

**Set a hard floor, not a lofty ceiling.**

- A target of six hours a day that nobody meets is worthless. A **minimum acceptable performance of five hours a day**, enforced, is worth far more. One firm arguing that five was too low was actually delivering an average of 3.7.
- Nobody progresses by doing the bare minimum. The floor is what you need to keep your job.
- **The only excuse for missing the floor is that there is no work in the firm** - and the lawyer must come to the owner and say so, so that work can be found.
- Consistently under five hours across a three-month period is a conversation about whether there is a role.
- **Only measure what can be controlled.**
- **Tailor performance criteria to circumstance, not experience.** Different practice groups support different rates and different hours. Do not put every three-year lawyer on the same target.

### 1.8 Price

**Price is the profit lever that has driven the Australian legal profession.** Between 1992 and 2007 mid-tier partner incomes rose from roughly $400,000 to over $1,000,000, and it was almost entirely price: fees rose 5% to 7% per year, compounding. Margins, utilisation and realisation stayed broadly constant across the period. Growth did not come from efficiency, productivity or greater client satisfaction.

**What is happening now:**

- Rack rates and realised rates both jumped in 2021, the COVID effect, and have continued rising.
- Smaller firms have surfed the wake of larger and boutique firms.
- Price has been the **default lever** for thirty years, because it flows straight to the bottom line. No extra recruitment, no extra rent.
- **Price sensitivity is returning** in smaller and regional markets. Practitioners report pushback, though whether that is gut feel or real data is unclear.
- Pushing price without producing more eventually produces client dissatisfaction, which then has to be managed.

**The practical move: tighten the rate spread.** A firm with partners at $500 and juniors at $250 has a 100% spread. Firms usually say they cannot move price because "the partners are already at $X" - focusing on the smallest group. The equation uses the **weighted average**. Leave the partners fixed and **bring the bottom up**: $250 becomes $275, $300 becomes $310. That moves the weighted average without touching the number clients most notice.

### 1.9 Margin

Margin is a **secondary** driver. You cannot save your way to success.

- Margin is largely offset by increases in the salary percentage and is dependent on structure.
- **Only compare your margin to your own firm's prior years.** Cross-firm margin comparisons are meaningless because structures differ.
- The cost/price squeeze is intensifying and **cost is currently winning**.
- Salaries keep pushing upward, driven by bracket creep and inflation.

Mid-tier margins have run roughly 31% to 37% over fifteen years, with a dip through 2017 to 2020 and recovery to around 36% to 37% since 2021.

### 1.10 Cash flow

Profit is not cash. The "money go round" has leaks and lock-ups at every stage:

- **Lock-up in WIP**: work done but not billed.
- **Leak at chargeable time**: work done but never recorded.
- **Loss at fees rendered**: write-downs between WIP and the bill.
- **Lock-up in debtors**: billed but not collected.
- **Loss at bad debts**: billed, never collected.
- **Loss on disbursements**: paid out, never recovered.

From the bank account, money goes out to equipment, drawings, tax, salaries and overheads. Funds come in from owners and loans. Every leak reduces what reaches the account.

**The controls:** capture all time; bill frequently; keep WIP under 40 days of production equivalent; chase slow payers; and understand that file **velocity** matters more than file volume.

### 1.11 Why firms do not change

Worth knowing, because you will meet all four in any partnership:

| Reason | How it sounds |
|---|---|
| **No urgency** | The problem is absorbable. The solutions are uncomfortable |
| **Inertia** | The larger the firm the greater the inertia. It is all a bit too hard |
| **Lack of ownership** | I am fine, others are the problem. Find someone performing worse and point |
| **Easier to wait it out** | It has turned around before. Keep my head down |

### 1.12 The non-financial predictors

These nine statements predict firm success more reliably than most financial metrics. Score your firm honestly:

1. Client satisfaction is a top priority of the firm.
2. The firm has no room for those who put their personal agenda ahead of the interests of clients or the firm.
3. Those who contribute most to the overall success of the firm are the most highly rewarded.
4. Management gets the best out of everybody in the firm.
5. Around here you are **required**, not just encouraged, to learn new skills.
6. The firm invests a significant amount of time in things that will pay off in the future.
7. People within the office always treat others with respect.
8. The quality of supervision of client files and projects is uniformly high.
9. The quality of professionals in our office is as high as can be expected.

## 2. The practical toolkit

### 2.1 Start with the two outcomes

Before any structural decision, decide what you actually want. Two outcomes are critical to contentment:

- **Earning what you want to earn.**
- **Experiencing the lifestyle you want.**

Whatever the outcome, it should be a **planned** outcome, not a residual one.

**Financial outcomes** range from nothing to more than $1,500,000 per partner per year. Profitability is determined by:

- Organised systems and procedures
- Client mix
- Pricing beyond cost
- Leverage structure
- Chargeable time per author per day
- Expense management
- Organisational culture
- Degree of specialisation
- Ownership (owners relative to revenue)
- Economies of scale
- Capacity for scope

As a business owner you should earn enough to cover **a fair salary for your time plus an appropriate profit**. Many practitioners fail to, either because they have insufficient work, or because they sell work below cost of production, or both.

**Lifestyle outcomes** are constrained by time. There are 24 hours in a day: how many will you sell, how many will you spend managing and building the business, and how many belong to your life outside the law? Three things to hold in mind while planning:

- **Most overheads in legal practice are fixed.** They do not vary with the volume of time or matters sold, so your targets have direct implications for your hourly or per-matter cost of production.
- Sole practitioners tend to improve profit by **compromising lifestyle**.
- Because overheads and available time are both fixed, **discounting below cost raises the cost of every other hour you have**.

### 2.2 Leverage for small and solo practices

Some practitioners will never employ anyone, and that is a viable model that has worked for decades. But it is limiting: you can only do so much yourself, so profit improvement then depends on working harder (costing lifestyle), charging more (hard in competitive markets), or cutting expenses (probably already minimal).

**Single-practitioner practice - you, a phone and an inexpensive office providing low-cost services for a fixed fee - is the most common model of practice and also the most expensive business model.**

For solos and barristers, think about leverage as **freeing your own time for high-value work**. That may mean help with clerical or paralegal work, research, admin, or even client management and communication.

**The structure of leverage matters as much as the quantity.** Typical returns per salary dollar:

| Level | Return per salary dollar |
|---|---|
| Entry level solicitors | ~$2.20 |
| Solicitors, 1 to 5 years | ~$3.50 |
| Associates | ~$2.70 |
| Special counsel / non-equity partners | ~$1.70 |

This is why **employed lawyers per equity partner correlates so strongly with net profit per equity partner**, and why firms that recruit graduates, train them and retain them for three to five years outperform firms whose employed lawyers are all senior and expensive. That effect is most pronounced where the client sets the fee or the firm quotes a fixed price.

### 2.3 Managing productivity

- **Lawyers who record their time are invariably better managers of their time.** Improvement follows measurement.
- **Lawyer budgets rarely improve mean performance.**
- **Minimum acceptable performance floors beat stretch targets** for profit improvement.
- **A minimum of 4 chargeable hours per day will improve profit in most firms.** (The Day 1 session argued for 5 as the floor in established firms.)
- **Teach new lawyers how to record time and why it still matters**, even in fixed fee offerings. Show them that productivity and balance can co-exist.

### 2.4 Price

- **Determine your cost of production.** Everything else follows from it.
- Regardless of pricing methodology, price should be **cost (including the principal's salary) plus a margin** that achieves your income and lifestyle aims.
- **Clients pay with time, uncertainty and cash.** Cash is not necessarily the most important to them.
- **Bill clients the way they want to be billed.**
- More work will be done for fixed fees, including contentious work.
- Because overheads are largely fixed, **any price increase flows directly to the bottom line**.
- **Raise price in the initial consultation.** Disclose likely cost fully and keep the client informed of any change to estimates.
- **Startups should bill fortnightly.**
- **If a bill may be larger than the client expects, telephone them before you send it.**

### 2.5 Improving profit and cash flow

- Profit is determined by price, productivity, leverage and expense control.
- **Set an annual profit budget** forecasting gross fees and expenses. **Monitor monthly.**
- Explore ways of turning **fixed costs into variable costs**.
- If you price on time, **capture all time spent on a file**.
- **Do not write time off.**
- **Bill frequently** so WIP stays below 40 days of production equivalent.
- **Chase slow payers.**
- On fixed fees, **take 50% up front** or agree billing intervals during the matter.
- **File velocity beats file volume.**
- **Market constantly**, to ensure sufficient work.
- Ensure all authors are hitting agreed productivity levels.
- **Model the impact of any discount before you offer it.**

### 2.6 Day-to-day management

Legal businesses that achieve their objectives are invariably **managed** businesses. Time must be allocated to:

- Determining objectives
- Developing systems that ensure quality and client satisfaction
- Marketing and practice development
- Accounting and financial management
- Staff management

This applies to sole practitioners as much as major firms. **Every firm needs a managing partner, even a firm of one** (see 2.11 for the job description).

**Technical proficiency will not produce business success.** Businesses succeed through consistently applied systems and processes that produce client experiences worth repeating and recommending.

Recommended reading: *The E-Myth Revisited*, Michael Gerber.

## 3. Fixed fee pricing

The threshold question before a firm moves to fixed fees: **are we going to charge more or less for the same service?**

- **Charging less** reduces profit significantly unless everyone works harder.
- **Charging more** assumes clients value certainty over total cost.
- **Charging the same** and simply changing the pricing mechanic makes little sense.

### 3.1 The profit argument

Profit is hard to grow by cutting expenses: most cannot be sourced more cheaply and nearly all are fixed against volume. Growing it through more hourly work means either people working harder for the same pay (which works but usually fails culturally) or acquiring resources.

**A worked comparison.** A practitioner wants $30,000 more profit. A lawyer rendering $350,000 a year on 1,000 billed hours could:

| Route | What it takes |
|---|---|
| Work harder | Bill another 85 hours. At 60% chargeable, that is **142 extra hours, about 18 days** |
| Add resources | At a 30% margin, render another $100,000. At $350/hour and 60% chargeable, about **476 person hours, or 60 days** |
| **Raise price 8.5%** | Charge **$380 instead of $350**. Expenses unchanged. **Same $30,000** |

Price is overwhelmingly the cheapest lever. Fixed fees are attractive partly because **it is easier to sell a fixed fee than an hourly rate**, and price increases are more palatable bundled into one.

**The unresolved question:** what happens when informed clients realise they are paying significantly more for the same service? The most successful firms achieve their results with **total price transparency and constant client communication**. A fixed price for a new client is entirely transparent. Managing the transition for existing clients who will now pay more is a harder conversation - unless the client has asked for fixed fees, in which case there is no issue at all.

### 3.2 Setting the fee

**Do fixed fees have to be more expensive to maintain profit? For most clients, yes.**

Think about 30 divorce matters priced on time. Each total differs.

- Charge everyone **the cheapest** price: profit collapses.
- Charge everyone **the highest** price: profit rises significantly, but 29 of 30 clients pay more.
- Charge everyone **the average**: half pay more, half pay less, profit is unchanged.

**To improve the profit outcome, the majority must pay more than they otherwise would have.**

Statistically, matter prices tend to be normally distributed. About 68% of a normal sample sits within one standard deviation of the mean and about 95% within two. To be nearly certain the firm is better off, every client would need to pay the mean plus two standard deviations - and you would need at least 30 matters to establish the distribution. Where the standard deviation is low this is manageable. Where it is high, it is a real problem.

**In practice** nobody sets fixed fees statistically. Practitioners use experience to set a fee reflecting the work given known circumstances. Where activity is near-certain - straightforward, non-contentious, transactional work - fixing fees is easy and has been done for years. Fixed fee litigation is harder.

**"One vexatious litigant and I'll go broke."** Probably not. If the vexatious litigant is a real phenomenon in your practice, they are inside the sample of 30 and are already priced in.

**The slippage problem.** Firms using time-based pricing average about **18% slippage** (write-downs and write-offs against recorded WIP), and that is inside an objective system. The potential for slippage in a subjective one is far greater. Many firms have done fixed price residential conveyancing below cost for years.

**Any sensible method needs a reference point**: what you charge on time, cost of production, years of experience, external assessors, or an approximation of what others charge. All work; some are riskier.

**Keep recording time regardless of pricing strategy.** The only way to make increasingly informed pricing decisions is to have accurate matter-by-matter cost of production at your fingertips. When practitioners moved from estimating fees at the end of a matter to recording time, profits rose sharply. Setting a fee *before* you start carries even more error potential than setting it at the end.

**The recommendation:** set fixed fees objectively, **compare the result against the value of recorded time**, and build corporate knowledge and estimating skill. Delay the timesheet burning ceremony for a few years.

**In short:** fixed fees work best in a practice with relatively homogeneous service offerings where prices are normally distributed with a **low standard deviation**. Practices with genuinely diverse clients and offerings produce wide price variation, and fixing fees there is brave.

### 3.3 Managing variations

Certainty is the whole appeal of a fixed fee, and certainty has no fine print. **Any variation from the quoted price creates dissatisfaction.**

The workable compromise is "certainty, subject to...". Set out exactly what you do for the fixed price, and price special circumstances through a **schedule of additional fixed fees** or on time. That reduces exposure to unquantifiable matters, but the magic of certainty loses some power.

**The bases for any variation must be made clear to the client before you start. If a variation becomes necessary, tell the client immediately.**

### 3.4 Litigation, party-party costs and interim billing

Litigation services in Australia have always been priced on **activity**: court scales, external assessors, event-based costing or time-based costing, all done during or after the event. Contingency fees are prohibited by regulators; success fees, where allowed, remain a function of activity rather than financial outcome.

Estimating activity and outcome at the outset is a significant change to practice. **Fee caps have become common**, particularly in very large matters - arguably a de facto fixed fee with the added prospect of a smaller one. Some of Australia's largest firms have had marketing success quoting one price for all of a corporate client's work for a year.

Two things to manage:

**Party-party costs.** Under a fixed fee, party-party recovery still requires negotiation or assessment. Since fixed fees generally produce a higher total price, **the gap between your fee and what is recovered from the other side will be wider**.

**If your client loses**, they must understand that your fixed fee does **not** include the other side's party-party costs. Obvious, and a rich source of misunderstanding.

If clients do not see value in paying significant costs in excess of what is recovered, they may prefer time-based pricing at realistic contemporary rates. Make sure the distinction between solicitor-own client costs and party-party costs is clearly understood at the outset.

### 3.5 "Value billing"

**A term to avoid.**

- If it means discussing with the client the value you bring relative to, say, a real estate agent or their likely profit, good luck. **Professionals should not base fees on a client's ability to pay.**
- Success depends on whether the client sees the relationship as partner-partner or master-servant. In the first case they will share the upside. In the second you will be seen as gouging.
- Start the conversation about relative worth and the client will ask "how long will it take you?" You are back to time.
- **If "value billing" is just a fashionable term for fixed fees, fine** - but do not use the phrase with clients or in promotional material.
- Clients come to you because you know more than they do. They generally do not want to come, they have to. **They expect you to know the fee, not negotiate it.**
- "Value" is subjective. Subjectivity creates uncertainty - the exact opposite of what fixed fees are meant to achieve. It also implies negotiation, and most price negotiations produce perceived winners and losers, not win-win.

**Whatever you decide, the discussion must include everyone involved in selling and collecting fees**: partners, solicitors, support staff and accounts staff. Any change needs wide support to succeed.

## 4. Managing cost of production

### 4.1 From price setter to price taker

Law firms used to set prices: decide the profit you want, tally expenses, set a chargeable hours budget, and the hourly rate falls out. Want more profit? Raise the rate.

That has ended. **Insourcing, digital disintermediation, commoditisation of repetitive high-margin work, increased client mobility, the shift from relationship purchasing to professional procurement, and the re-entry of global accounting firms** have made most firms price takers in a competitive market.

**Profitability comes down to price, volume and cost of production.** Price will always matter, but in commoditising markets **cost of production has become the most significant lever**.

### 4.2 Why lawyers should understand cost

Few lawyers understand cost, whether the cost of a matter or the cost of producing an hour of chargeable time. Many firms have financial managers and BI software doing the maths behind the scenes, but **in firms of all sizes every lawyer should understand cost and what they can do to minimise it without damaging the client experience**.

Firms trade at margins below 20% and above 60%. Price explains some of the gap; **managing cost explains a great deal of it**.

### 4.3 Three conclusions

**1. Leveraged practices produce legal work significantly cheaper than sole operators.** This matters most for non-time-priced work and where the client sets the fee. It is the mechanism behind the correlation between employed lawyers per equity partner and net profit per equity partner.

**2. The most immediate lever for reducing cost is available chargeable time.** Salaries are negotiated annually and non-salary costs vary little with volume, so **the greater the volume against a fixed cost base, the greater the margin**. Without the lever of price, productive chargeable effort becomes integral to profit. "Working smarter not harder" is admirable, but working harder has its merits.

**3. The more junior and cheaper your capable employed lawyers, the greater the effect of leverage on cost.** Firms that recruit graduates, train them and retain them for three to five years will be more profitable than firms whose employed lawyers are all senior.

**The caveat.** Profitability is a small part of strategy, though its absence sharpens focus. Regardless of cost of production, keep investing in diverse partnerships that accommodate part-time equity partners, in training and retaining graduates, and in improving the client experience.

**The discipline:** find out what the client wants, what they will pay for it, and then manage your costs accordingly through structure and productivity.

### 4.4 Understanding cost of production

Practical points from the session:

- **Cost of production attaches to an individual, not to an activity.** That is the opportunity to lower it: move the work to the right person.
- **To operate with a profit motive you cannot do work for less than cost of production.**
- **Emphasise file velocity rather than volume**, particularly for fixed fee work.
- **Understand the effect of discounting** before you agree to it. Because overheads are fixed, a discount comes straight off the bottom line and raises the cost of every other hour.

### 4.5 Pricing options

| Cost based billing | Value based billing |
|---|---|
| Hourly rate | Fixed fee |
| Blended hourly rate | Retrospective fee based on value |
| Fixed fee plus contingency | Retainer |
| | Unit fee |

**Marketing aspects of price:**

- **Service quality dimensions** - what the client is actually buying.
- **No surprises** - the single most important pricing behaviour.
- **Packaging** - how the offer is bundled and presented.
- Moving from cost based to value based: seek higher dollar realisation, raise the value of the service, and apply the **golden rule of reasonableness**.

## 5. The office manual

A documented office manual is what turns technical proficiency into a repeatable business. Use this index as a starting structure and populate it over time. Even a firm of one benefits from writing down how things are done.

**1. Introduction** - practice history; short, mid and long term objectives; practice standards; confidentiality.

**2. Staff** - structure by department (titles and job descriptions); staff private addresses and telephones; performance management and feedback; in-house training; appearance and grooming; co-operation.

**3. Conditions of employment** - hours of business; hours of work; wages; wage reviews; period and method of payment; holidays; Christmas break; sick leave; notification of absence or late arrival; leave without pay; long service leave; parental leave; other special leave; time off; time in lieu; notice of termination; probation; overtime; injury; word processing time breaks; tea breaks and tea room upkeep; telephone etiquette; standard of dress; smoking; personal phone calls; equipment for personal use; lock-up; letter of engagement; work health and safety and rehabilitation.

**4. Travel expenses policy** - expense claim forms; meals, entertainment, accommodation, motor vehicles; after-hours travel home.

**5. Staff meetings** - team meetings; support staff meetings; daily pre-start meetings; day's end meetings.

**6. Office systems and procedures** - outgoing correspondence; facsimiles; email; billing; phone policy (for example, return messages within 3 hours); filing documents; disposal of documents; where to get things and stationery orders; short dial numbers; interviewing techniques; client contact standards.

**7. Standard forms and formats** - quality control of outgoing correspondence: bills, letters, reminders, thank you cards.

**8. Quality management programme** - telephone; reception; practitioner support and staff attitude; total quality management (measurement, improvement, teamwork, data-based decisions); complaints procedures.

**9. General** - advertising and promotion; accommodation and security; parking; bank used; mail address; preferred services; library; lawyers' offices; conference rooms; cleaning; sterilisation; reception area.

## 6. The managing partner role

**Every firm needs one, including a firm of one.** Use this as a job description if you are appointing someone, or as a checklist of what you must do yourself.

### 6.1 Overall responsibility

The managing partner is responsible for the management of the firm and for guiding the business decisions of the partnership to maximise profitability and business success. In general they initiate and direct implementation of policies set by the management committee on financial, operational and administrative matters, and establish procedures to ensure all professional and administrative support activities run efficiently.

### 6.2 Primary responsibilities

- Day-to-day direction of operational activities, including all administrative support functions.
- Instituting finance and accounting policies and procedures to increase profitability, through timely and accurate monthly financial statements, variance analyses and other management reports.
- Functioning as **business and financial counsellor to the partnership**.
- Maintaining, through the bookkeeper, the firm's systems of accounting, timekeeping, billing and collections.
- Directing the firm's planning process: overseeing business plans, budgets and forecasts; preparing long and short range plans in accordance with objectives, strategies and professional standards defined by the partnership.
- Developing strategic plans with the partners for growth, including analysing the feasibility of new practice areas or geographic markets and analysing staffing needs.
- Analysing staffing, **utilisation and realisation**, including lawyer productivity and profitability, and helping the partnership develop policies to optimise both.
- Functioning as business liaison and adviser to the firm's committees.
- Overseeing the firm's data processing and applications development; setting IT priorities.
- Directing lawyer support functions: secretarial, word processing, reproduction, messenger service, purchasing, library, reception and telephones; investigation, acquisition, disposition and safeguarding of office equipment.
- **Identifying areas of potential cost reduction and efficiency improvement**; maintaining appropriate internal controls and auditing procedures.
- Planning the use of space and equipment: relocations, leasehold improvements, maintenance, and relationships with the landlord, other tenants and sub-tenants; directing outside contractors.
- Supervising library administration and subscription services.
- Reviewing the firm's insurance and other benefits and making recommendations.
- Preparing special analyses and directing special projects.

## 7. Business Planning

### 7.1 Two questions before the plan

- **What does success look like to you?**
- **How do you view the market?**

Everything downstream is an answer to those two. Skip them and the plan becomes a budget with a cover page.

### 7.2 The structure of the plan

**The business**

- Business structure and ownership
- Premises
- Management
- Key personnel
- Services
- Operations
- Risk management

**The market**

- Target markets
- Proposed clients
- SWOT analysis
- Competitor analysis
- Promotion strategies

**The future**

- Vision
- Purpose
- Objectives and goals
- Achieving action

**The finances**

- Key financial objectives
- Start-up costs
- Pricing strategy
- Profit and loss budget
- Fee earner budgets
- Cash flow projections

### 7.3 The four-step framework

A business plan reduces to four questions:

1. **Where are you today?**
2. **Where do you want to be in 12 months?**
3. **What strategies get you from A to B?**
4. **What actions sit under each strategy?**

**Pick only three or four strategies.** Plans fail from overload, not from underambition.

### 7.4 Key Result Objectives - the shape of the practice

Map the practice across three dimensions, today and as you want it:

| Dimension | Questions |
|---|---|
| **Service mix** | What work do you do? Commercial, disputes, insurance, family, estates? What proportion of each? |
| **Client mix** | Business, individual, government? Listed companies have very different needs to consumers |
| **Practice mix** | Fees budget; accounts receivable days; fees introduced; client growth; introduced fees versus supervised fees; profitability by segment |

Map where you are now, map where you want to be, and the gap **is** the plan.

### 7.5 Three planning tools

**SWOT.** Strengths and weaknesses are **internal** and you control them. Opportunities and threats are **external** and you can only respond to them. Document at least three of each, and revisit in 12 months.

A realistic small-firm SWOT:

| | |
|---|---|
| **Strengths** - firm growth path; good annuity client base; practice management systems and support | **Weaknesses** - lack of awareness among prospective clients |
| **Opportunities** - market segment poised for rapid growth; cross-selling services to existing clients | **Threats** - AI; larger firms discounting; price sensitivity in clients; role of procurement increasing |

The most common small-firm weakness is being the **best kept secret**: the only people who can speak to what you offer are the people you already work with.

On opportunities, invest in **market knowledge**. Industry data is far more accessible than it once was (IBISWorld, Company360 and similar). Understand your sector's trends, what your clients are going through, and what is coming.

**Portfolio management (the growth/share matrix).** Plot services or client segments on attractiveness against growth:

| | High attractiveness | Low attractiveness |
|---|---|---|
| **High growth** | **Stars** - high growth, high attractiveness. Challenging work people want to do. Invest | **Question marks** - possibly growing, but you are not passionate about the work or the clients. Interrogate whether you want this in the business |
| **Low growth** | **Cash cows** - bread and butter. Not high growth, but they pay on time, they are easy, they do not drain you. These keep you going | **Dogs** - low growth, low attractiveness. No reason to keep doing this work |

**Attractiveness is not only financial.** It includes whether the work is enjoyable, whether it develops people, and whether the client is easy to deal with. Ask three questions: is there demand, do I have a strength in it, and am I passionate about it?

**A caveat for regional and relationship-based practices.** Divesting "dog" clients is easy in theory and hard in a small community where you have local relationships and reputation to manage. Be realistic about what you can actually exit.

**The experience curve and three horizons.**

*The experience curve*: the more often you do something, the faster and better you get, and procedure time falls towards an asymptotic expert level. This is the financial argument for **specialisation**: you can do more of that work in the same time, and you can position yourself as genuinely understanding a client type or matter type. It also explains why specialists move to fixed fees - getting faster no longer reduces your income.

*Three horizons*: run three parallel time frames, with only one or two initiatives in each.

| Horizon | Frame | Typical content |
|---|---|---|
| **Horizon 1** - operators extend the core | 0 to 12 months | Set up the technology; find clients; build the website; update collateral to reflect a new position |
| **Horizon 2** - business builders develop new opportunities | 1 to 5 years | Build a referral network or business association relationship; enter an adjacent service area |
| **Horizon 3** - visionaries create viable options | Long term | Succession; partner health and capacity; what the business becomes when you stop |

### 7.6 Twelve ways to build top line growth

Growth gets harder and more expensive as you move left to right. Most practitioners skip straight to new clients and new products and neglect the first column, which is the cheapest.

**Existing clients**

1. **Increase fee rates.** A striking number of practitioners are still honouring rates set years ago because they have never been comfortable raising them. At minimum, index annually.
2. **Improve realisation.** Unlock the value you already provide.
3. **Deepen.** Win more of the same type of work; become a dominant provider of that service.
4. **Diversify.** Increase the range of services and products provided to existing clients.

**New clients**

5. New clients won through the sales efforts of existing team members.
6. New clients won by new sales channels.
7. Increase the quality, size and frequency of referrals from existing sources.
8. Expand the pool of referral sources.
9. Acquire clients (lateral hires, practice acquisition).

**New products**

10. Commercialise existing products or services, including charging for things you currently give away.
11. Develop new products or services.

**And**

12. **Reduce, stop or divest.** Getting rid of the wrong clients is a legitimate growth strategy.

**The design method for the first column:** put the client in the centre of a page and ask what they need by way of legal services **over time**. A family law practice that also does property and estate planning can follow a client from divorce, through the next relationship's prenuptial agreement, through property, into estate planning. Build the service range around the client's life, not around your own practice areas.

## 8. Ethics

### 8.1 What legal ethics is not

**It is not morality.** Morality is about being the best version of you. **Legal ethics has a narrow, specific purpose.** The two overlap but they are not the same thing, and they can conflict.

**It is not moral purity.** Sir Frank Kitto in *Ziems v The Prothonotary of the Supreme Court of NSW* (1957) 97 CLR 279: it cannot be that every proof of human frailty disqualifies a practitioner. The ends are lofty, but they are pursued "with man, not with paragons". The test is whether you are a **fit and proper person**, not whether you are blameless.

**It is not slavish rule-following.** Two problems with treating ethics as compliance: the rules do not cover everything, and many of the rules are **discretionary**, which means they require your judgment.

### 8.2 What legal ethics is

**A relationship with the administration of justice.**

The paramount duty rule ([ASCR r 3.1](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.3)): a solicitor's duty to the court and the administration of justice is **paramount** and prevails to the extent of inconsistency with any other duty.

**Every rule that follows is read in the light of that one.** When a practitioner is before a tribunal, whatever the charge - breach of confidentiality, discourtesy, misuse of trust money - once the facts are established the tribunal will bring it back to the paramount duty: your actions damaged the proper processes of law, cast a shadow over legal outcomes, or damaged public confidence in the system you serve.

**What is the administration of justice?** Not just the courts. It is the whole system: the laws, the courts, the profession, and every institution that makes up the system of justice, of which you are an officer. Your job is to make sure it functions at its best.

Two judicial formulations worth keeping:

- *Fagan J in Croke*: as a solicitor and therefore an officer of the court, the first duty is to uphold the law and to support the integrity of the authorities and institutions through which it is applied.
- *Ipp*: the paramount duty is justified because the court represents the public interest in the administration of justice. The lawyer's highest loyalties are at the same time the most intangible - a loyalty that runs not to persons but to procedures and institutions, imposing a trusteeship for the integrity of the fundamental processes of government and self-government on which society depends.

**You may criticise the system.** Criticise legislation, criticise political decisions. The purpose must be to make it better, not to undermine it.

**Your relationships with the four Cs** - courts, clients, colleagues and the communities you serve - either sustain or detract from that core relationship.

### 8.3 The one thing to hold onto

When you have scratched your head, called the ethics hotline and spoken to colleagues you respect, and you are still unsure, this is your north point:

> **Would my actions in some way damage the system I represent?**

### 8.4 Duty versus morality - the worked case

A 10-year-old boy was sexually assaulted. The perpetrator pleaded guilty. On the Crown's appeal against sentence, and days before the appeal, the now-convicted offender told his lawyer he was **HIV positive**. The child had not been medically examined.

The moral pull is obvious: adults protect children. The professional position is confidentiality.

**Where the room usually splits.** Criminal defence lawyers divide. One half: my job is to protect my client; if the client instructs me not to disclose, that is the end of it. The other half: it is a child, how can you say nothing?

**Options considered and rejected:**

- **Withdraw from the retainer.** It helps nobody. It does not help the child, and it does not help a vulnerable client who needs representation. There is no just cause, and withdrawing would itself likely breach [ASCR r 13](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.13). **Moral squeamishness is not just cause.**
- **Rely on the duty to the court to disclose.** Part of the duty to the administration of justice *requires* protecting client confidentiality. It does not cut the other way here.

**The rule that applies.** [ASCR r 9.1](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.9) states the confidentiality obligation. [Rule 9.2](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.9) lists six exceptions, two of which are the public interest exceptions:

- **r 9.2.4** - to prevent the commission of a serious offence
- **r 9.2.5** - to prevent **imminent serious physical harm**

**Is there still a future harm?** Yes, twice over. The child could develop untreated illness, and the child could inadvertently transmit it to others. That is the public interest.

**What the lawyer did.** She discussed it with the client first, which you should always do. The client refused consent. She then telephoned the DPP and told them **just enough**: that the child should be medically examined. One sentence. No other disclosure.

**The court's view.** The appeal judge commended her for her appreciation of her ethical obligations, noting that the public safety and future harm exception, and any reporting duties arising from it, are not easy areas of law to apply. The child was examined and was negative.

**The second question, and the important one.** If she had said nothing, would she have been wrong? **No.** The exceptions are **discretionary**: you *may* disclose. It was her judgment call.

**Practical additions from the discussion:**

- **Always have the conversation with the client first** about whether they consent to disclosure.
- **Document** the conversation and the client's response, including how terse it was.
- Weigh the **nature of the information** and the significance of the detriment it would cause others.

### 8.5 Instructions - the three-box test

[ASCR r 7](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.7) requires you to put the client in a position to make **informed choices**. Where there is more than one option, spell out the pros and cons of each. The client chooses. You may recommend option A; if the client prefers option B and the choice is fully informed, you have done your job.

[ASCR r 8](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.8): you must only accept and follow instructions that are **lawful, proper and competent**.

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| **Lawful** | The easy one. You cannot assist someone to break the law |
| **Proper** | Am I being asked to do something contrary to my broader ethical duties or the conduct rules? |
| **Competent** | Am I satisfied the person understands the instructions and their consequences? In a commercial context, is this person **authorised** to give them? |

**If all three boxes are ticked, the rule says you must follow.** At that point any moral squeamishness becomes irrelevant.

**The client who constantly changes instructions.** This raises a **competence** flag. Are they changing on good advice, or on something they read online? Start with a frank discussion about the outcomes they want and the best route to them. If instructions change so frequently that they cannot be followed, that becomes very difficult to continue with.

**In litigation there is an additional discipline.** You certify reasonable prospects of success, and you are personally liable for that certification. If changed instructions mean the matter no longer has reasonable prospects, you must withdraw the certification - which gives you a concrete basis for a hard conversation with a flaky client.

### 8.6 Withdrawing from a retainer

You may only withdraw with **just cause and reasonable notice** ([ASCR r 13.1.3](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.13)). This reflects the doctrine of entire contract: you cannot withdraw without just cause once you have started. The cab rank principle formally binds barristers, but the practical effect for solicitors is similar.

**Rule 13.4** now defines just cause to include:

- instructions requiring, or any circumstance where continuation would cause, a breach of the solicitor's ethical duties and professional obligations;
- any circumstance where the solicitor is no longer able to act in the client's best interests;
- any circumstance where a client has not provided all information requested that is required to fulfil the solicitor's **statutory obligations**.

That third limb was added in light of AML/CTF.

**You do not turn clients away because they make you morally squeamish or because you do not like them.** Your paramount duty is to ensure they receive all the protections the law affords them.

### 8.7 Where complaints actually come from

The Legal Services Commissioner's top complaint categories:

1. **Negligence**
2. **Communication**
3. **Ethical matters** (up sharply this year)
4. Overcharging
5. Misleading conduct
6. Delay
7. General costs complaints
8. Not following instructions
9. Acting in a conflict
10. Costs disclosure

Negligence and communication trade places at the top year on year. **Combine the three costs categories and they are still effectively number one.**

**"Ethical matters" means:** ceasing to act when you should not; communicating with another lawyer's client (which you never should); breaching confidentiality; personal conduct; abuse of process; and other ethical matters.

**LawCover's negligence claims** are led by **conveyancing**, then **litigation**, then **family law**. Reasons for claim are led by poor communication, then not knowing the law adequately, then document problems and system problems.

**Two operational takeaways:**

- **Manage communication expectations explicitly.** Clients now expect immediate email responses and you cannot deliver that. Set aside dedicated time each day for the important ones and tell clients what your response standard is.
- **AI hallucination is still producing disciplinary matters weekly.** Legal-specific research tools still hallucinate, reportedly in the range of 17% to 33% on factual matters. Verify every citation.

### 8.8 AML/CTF and the ethical friction

The Tranche 2 obligations commenced 1 July 2026 and created genuine tension with the confidentiality duty: you must in some circumstances report a client to AUSTRAC **and not tell them**.

Two conduct rules were amended in consequence: **r 8** (accepting and following instructions) and **r 13** (adding r 13.4 on just cause).

The confidentiality rule already contains an exception for disclosure **compelled by law** ([ASCR r 9.2](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0244#sec.9)), which accommodates the reporting obligation.

**The practical response: front-end your advices.** At the very beginning of any conversation with a client about their matter, set out what your obligations are should they disclose certain things. That is how you avoid the worst of the conflict.

See the **Anti-Money Laundering** guide for the substantive obligations.

### 8.9 Use the ethics hotline

The Law Society of NSW operates an ethics enquiry service staffed by experts, free to members. Email tends to be more reliable than telephone.

**The "ick" rule, which is not in the conduct rules but should be: if it feels wrong, call.** It costs nothing and it means you are not making the call alone.

## 9. Business Development and Marketing

### 9.1 Strategy first

Three questions define the strategy:

- **Where will I play?** Target market.
- **How will I compete?** Competitive strategy.
- **Why will I win?** Compelling proposition.

**The scale of the field:** roughly 16,000 law firms, 13,000 to 14,000 sole practitioners and 90,000 solicitors in Australia. Why would someone choose you over any of them?

**A useful exercise:** look at who you compete against, and separately at who you would aspire to be like. Study both. How do they advertise? What associations do they join? What pro bono work do they do? Where do they show up?

### 9.2 Brand and competitive positioning

Marketing's role is to promote **those attributes which are most important to a defined market**. So first decide which attributes you are competing on: price, quality, speed, service.

**The tier insight.** Plot any market on price against quality and you get distinct tiers. People buy a brand promise and what it says about them - which is why one puffer jacket sells for $1,500 and another for $15 when both keep you warm. **Most buyers will move only one tier up or one tier down.** They do not jump from the bottom tier to the top.

The same happens in professional services: "I only use the big firms" is a purchase of cachet, not capability.

**The four Ps** applied to legal services: product becomes **service and skills**; price becomes **value**; place and promotion are where and how you show up.

### 9.3 Positioning your practice - the professional service spectrum

Four models, from the Harvard Business Review framework. Each implies a different business, not just a different market.

| | **Commodity** | **Procedure** | **Grey hair** | **Rocket science** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Client need** | Solving routine, often simple problems | Implementing a complex project with several interrelated parts | Developing a major, ill-defined and unclear solution with the client | Addressing a unique problem with major consequences |
| **Selling proposition** | Low cost | Systematic and consistent approach | Track record of finding and solving similar problems | Ability to solve complex challenges |
| **Organisational capability** | Efficient delivery processes | Methodical, systematic | Rigorous analytical ability | State of the art theory |
| **Personnel requirements** | Focused execution against specific requirements | Following procedures and complex processes famously | Converting specific experience to judgment | Applying theoretical and conceptual tools to devise innovative solutions |
| **Profit levers** | High **leverage**, high **utilisation**, low rate | | | High **rate**, low leverage, low utilisation |

**What this means practically:**

- **Commodity** work (volume wills, straightforward conveyancing) means your job becomes **managing people**, not doing the work. It suits automation and AI investment. Low margin per matter, high volume, high leverage, high utilisation. Your investment goes into technology and process.
- **Rocket science** work means the business is **you**. It is very hard to hire for, hard to bring juniors through, and **succession is a real question mark**. You build alongside other experts rather than beneath yourself.
- Most practices sit in **procedure** or **grey hair** in between. Be intentional about which, because the setup of the business changes accordingly.

**Door law** - taking every matter that walks in - can be fruitful early. The problem is you end up with a book of clients you may not want, and those clients refer clients like themselves. **Be purposeful.**

### 9.4 Market segmentation

Break the market down on any of these axes and decide where you want to play:

- **Client industries**
- **Work types**
- **Life stage of client or trigger event** - the moment they are most likely to buy
- **Management style and risk profile**
- **Location** (geo-targeting)
- **Price versus quality** preference
- **Sector** - public or private
- **Type of buyer** - organisation or individual
- **Nature of the decision-making unit and buying process** - panel driven? procurement driven?
- **Client size**

Then separate the **rational** attributes from the **emotional** ones, because both drive the purchase.

**Build an avatar.** Draw a picture of your ideal client. If you do estate litigation on a no-win-no-fee basis and you want under-represented beneficiaries, their pain points and demographics are entirely different from those of the executors. Decide which, then write for that person.

### 9.5 Sell the hole, not the drill

People do not buy features. **They buy a feeling and an outcome.**

Nobody wants a drill. They want a hole. Actually they want their children's photographs on a shelf. The drill is just the mechanism.

Applied to legal services, what you sell is **peace of mind**, or the ability to keep running a business while a dispute is handled, or protection of something the client built. The service itself is just the service.

**So: in everything you write about yourself, express what the client actually gets, not what you do.** Most practitioners write pure fact with no benefit and no emotion, and it does not land. The most successful lawyers are the ones who can genuinely connect and show empathy.

### 9.6 The four pillars of practice growth

A sequence, not a menu. Each pillar has a job.

| | **1. Strategy** | **2. Marketing** | **3. Business development** | **4. Client experience management** |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Question** | WHERE will I play? HOW will I compete? WHY will I win? | AWARENESS (web, social, media); UNDERSTANDING (articles, industry engagement); CONSIDERATION and BELIEF (one to one, evidence) | Triggers and filters; discussion; scope / price / pitch; go / no go | Enter; engage; exit |
| **Outcome** | FOCUS | AWARE | INSTRUCT | Extend and defend |

**The critical distinction:**

- **Marketing is one to many.** It is messaging and positioning. **Its purpose is to create demand** so that demand comes to you.
- **Business development is one to one.** It is the connection piece: meeting people, shaking hands, targeting a specific company or person, turning that into an ongoing relationship.

**The funnel behind it:** awareness ("I have heard of you") to consideration ("I know what you do") to any use ("I will give you a go") to much use ("I will use you again and tell others").

### 9.7 Building profile: visibility plus reputation

> **Networks + Reputation = Social capital**

**WHO to build with:**

- Market sectors
- Communities
- Professional groups and learning
- Personal networks
- Homogeneous networks versus diversified networks
- Personal enjoyment

**HOW to build:**

1. Being easy to work with
2. Writing: contributing to articles and content
3. Speaking: in-house and external
4. Digital: social media engagement
5. Acknowledgement and testimonials
6. Attending events
7. Hosting
8. Awards and directory listings
9. Research: study groups, proprietary research
10. Paid: advertising, sponsorships
11. Being the "glue" person in networks

**The governing principle: fish where the fish are.** If you were trying to catch a trout, would you cast your line in the ocean?

### 9.8 Networks

**The map exercise.** Take a blank sheet. Draw **everyone you know** - school, university, community groups, professional life. Then separately map **which networks your clients belong to**. Then look at where the two overlap. That overlap is where your business development effort belongs.

**Wide and weak beats narrow and strong.** You do not need strong ties with everyone you refer to or who refers to you. A **broad, diverse network of weaker ties** - people you meet occasionally and stay connected with - produces more opportunity than a small tight one.

**On LinkedIn:** its genuine value is staying connected with people you would otherwise never keep in touch with. Use it as a connection maintenance tool rather than a broadcast channel.

**On referrals:** referral should be your **number one marketing channel**, ahead of any form of advertising. Work out who comes into contact with your type of client regularly, then work backwards. A personal injury practice pairs naturally with a physiotherapist.

**The Dale Carnegie principle (1937), which still holds:** you can make more friends in two months by becoming interested in other people than you can in two years by trying to get people interested in you.

**The foundation is being confident about five things:**

1. Describing **why** you do what you do
2. Describing the firm or your practice
3. Describing your market - who you help
4. Describing the types of problem you enjoy solving
5. Meeting everyone you can

### 9.9 Top of mind, top of tongue

It is one thing to be **top of mind** - "what was that lawyer's name?" It is another to be **top of tongue** - someone hears about a problem, thinks of you, and **says your name out loud**. Thinking about you is not enough.

**The 12 by 12 by 12 rule** (from BNI, and a good discipline for any networking event):

- **From 12 feet away** people judge whether you are early or late, dressed appropriately, and whether you look like you want to be there.
- **From 12 inches away** they judge congruence: do you match the person they saw from a distance? Are you looking them in the eye or over their shoulder?
- **Then the 12 words you use.**

**Work out your 12 words.** Not "I'm a lawyer", not "I do family law", not "I'm a patent attorney" - those mean nothing to anyone and people glaze over. **Describe what you deliver, not what you do.** What is the win for a client?

And choose the room. Great rapport at a wedding is wasted if you deliver your pitch to the children's table. **Choosing a market well is part of the skill.**

### 9.10 From pipeline to platform

The shift in how business development works:

| Pipeline thinking | Platform thinking |
|---|---|
| Win more deals | Build a stronger ecosystem |
| Optimise conversion rates | Deepen relationship quality |
| Track client billings | Track network resilience |
| Pitch and sell | Orchestrate and connect |
| Individual "rainmakers" | Collaborative conductors |

### 9.11 From rainmaker to modern business developer

| Rainmaker era (1980s-2000s) | Contemporary era (2010s onwards) |
|---|---|
| Charm and personality | Expertise and empathy |
| Wine, dine, entertain | Listen, understand, collaborate |
| Information asymmetry | Transparent, informed buyers |
| Elevator pitches | Authentic conversations |
| Quick wins | Long term relationships |
| Power imbalance | Equal partnerships |
| "Us" versus "them" | Collaboration and inclusion |

The practical consequence: the elevator pitch is dead. Clients arrive informed, and the differentiator is whether you can listen and understand rather than whether you can perform.

### 9.12 Client pathways and proactive engagement

Five touchpoints across the life of a matter. The focusing question at each: **how do I get, and give, the most from every touchpoint of my known networks?**

1. **Entice** - how you invest in your expertise, networks and reputation before the client arrives.
2. **Enter** - what you do in first client meetings and at the start of a matter.
3. **Engage** - the experience you create as you deliver, day to day.
4. **Exit** - how you end specific projects, and reflect on the promise made and the value created.
5. **Extend** - what happens beyond projects and matters to build your expertise, networks and reputation.

### 9.13 Activating advocacy - the exit conversation

**At the exit of every matter there is a moment that matters.** It is the point at which you can have a high-value conversation with a client about their experience.

The intent of that conversation is to reflect on the **promise** and the **value created**, and to create a ramp onto the next stage of the relationship.

**Keep a kit of good questions.** Questions that generate further conversation, and that you feel comfortable asking. Two categories to prepare:

- What questions could you ask a client? A referrer?
- How do you ask a friend for work, or for an introduction?

**Relationships, and trust, are built by scaffolding out from the current state.** Not by leaping.

### 9.14 A rainmaker kit for every lawyer

Build good BD habits from day one:

**The five things to be able to describe:**

1. What you enjoy
2. Your firm
3. Your market
4. A hero story
5. Meet everyone you can

**The four structural habits:**

1. A **monthly BD plan**
2. A **weekly activity plan**
3. **Blend personal interests** into it, so it is sustainable
4. **Have easy measures**

The point is to **encourage activity**, not to build a perfect system.

### 9.15 Prioritisation - importance versus urgency

The standard matrix, and the reason most BD never happens: BD is almost always **important but not urgent**, which means it gets displaced by client work forever unless you schedule it.

| | Urgent | Not urgent |
|---|---|---|
| **Important** | **1.** Do it now | **2.** Decide when to do it |
| **Not important** | **4.** Delegate it | **3.** Dump it |

BD lives in box 2. **Decide when.** Put it in the diary.

### 9.16 Client portfolio analysis

Ten measures to run over your client base, at least annually:

1. Clients providing the top 20%, 50% and 80% of revenue over the past 3 years
2. Change in the client base over 3 years - entrants, exits, and the "planks" of the practice
3. Clients won, and why
4. Clients lost, and why
5. Number of different services provided to top clients
6. Segmentation of clients by geography, structure and business type
7. Origination of top clients
8. Fee forecast for each client
9. Satisfaction measure for each client (for example a score out of 10)
10. Advocacy measure for each client and referral source (for example number of referrals)

Measures 5, 9 and 10 are the ones most firms never run and the ones that most reliably identify growth available from the client base you already have.

### 9.17 Practical marketing points from the session

- **Pitching to procurement or government:** if you do not answer the questions as posed, use their wording, and attach every document requested, the submission goes in the bin regardless of how good the offer is or how competitive the price. Compliance comes before persuasion.
- **Set layered revenue targets.** A good practice: at the start of each year each partner plans total revenue for the year, broken down into revenue from **existing clients**, revenue that must come from **brand new clients**, and **blue sky** opportunities. That beats adding a percentage to last year and working out later how to get there.
- **Time on the business is hard to find and is the thing that compounds.**

## 10. Action checklist

**Financial management**

- [ ] Build the profit driver equation for your practice in a spreadsheet and model scenarios
- [ ] Determine your cost of production per hour and per matter type
- [ ] Set an annual profit budget and monitor it monthly
- [ ] Set a **hard floor** for chargeable hours and enforce it; only measure what people control
- [ ] Review the rate spread and bring the bottom up
- [ ] Keep WIP under 40 days of production equivalent
- [ ] Model the effect of any discount before offering it

**Systems**

- [ ] Build the office manual against the index at section 5, one section at a time
- [ ] Write or adopt the managing partner job description, and allocate the time to do it
- [ ] Document file opening, billing, communication standards and file closure

- [ ] Answer the two questions: what does success look like, and how do you view the market
- [ ] Map service mix, client mix and practice mix as they are and as you want them
- [ ] Complete a SWOT with at least three entries per quadrant
- [ ] Plot services and clients on the portfolio matrix
- [ ] Set one or two initiatives in each of the three horizons
- [ ] Pick **three or four** strategies, not more

- [ ] Set and communicate a response-time standard for client communication
- [ ] Verify every AI-generated citation
- [ ] Programme the Law Society ethics line into your phone

**Business development**

- [ ] Build the exit conversation into your matter close process
