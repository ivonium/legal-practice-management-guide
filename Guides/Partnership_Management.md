# Partnership Management - Practical Guide

Entry, performance, compensation and exit. Stated as at 8 September 2026.

---

## 1. The framing that changes everything

**Partnership is a risk management exercise.**

It is not the best thing ever, and it is not something you could not possibly imagine practising without. **You are going into business with other people to spread risk.**

That single reframing explains most of what follows, and in particular why differential compensation is dangerous in a small firm.

**If you introduce too much differential in compensation, you reintroduce risk into the partnership - which makes you ask why you are in a partnership at all.**

The whole point of equal shares in a small firm is that someone is up in one year and down in another, and **over time it sorts itself out**. That is risk-sharing working as designed. The moment you start adjusting for individual performance, you remove the mechanism.

---

## 2. Entry - the two models

There are two broad ways people come into ownership.

| | **Traditional** | **Lockstep** |
|---|---|---|
| **What happens** | You **purchase** a share or interest, paying for goodwill | You make a **capital contribution** and join a points-based profit share |
| **Who uses it** | Most small and mid-sized firms | Larger firms, and an increasing number of smaller, profitable, focused practices |
| **What you get** | An asset that grows and is ultimately saleable | **Your ticket to get on the ride.** When you leave you get back what you put in |
| **What you are paying for** | Goodwill | **Foregone profit income over time** |

---

## 3. Valuation - the traditional route

### 3.1 The formula

> **Value of a firm = Goodwill + Net assets**

**Goodwill comes from the profit and loss account. Net assets is a balance sheet issue.**

### 3.2 The golden rule of goodwill

> **Does the firm generate profits beyond a fair market salary to the owners?**

**If it does not, you are buying a job.** There is no goodwill. The firm has to be a profit-making machine.

### 3.3 How the calculation works

1. **Go back three years** in the profit and loss.
2. Look at **professional fees** and **profit**.
3. Make the necessary **adjustments**.
4. **Deduct a fair market salary for each partner.** In the worked example this was set at $150,000 per partner, and that is arguably light. **Use a genuinely fair figure, not a convenient one.**
5. What remains is **adjusted EBIT after salaries**.
6. **Weight the three years** to arrive at a sustainable annual profit figure. In the example, $731,000.
7. If that number is positive and durable, **goodwill exists**.

### 3.4 What someone should pay for it

**This is where most valuations go wrong.**

An accountant will typically produce a **capitalisation rate or multiplier**, usually between **2.5 and 3 times**. **That figure does not take into account the unique components of the particular firm.**

**The better methodology: a purchaser should be able to recoup their investment in post-tax cash within a defined period.** To do that you have to look at the firm's actual cash flow, tax rates, borrowing costs and minority interest.

**The rule of thumb that should make you cautious:**

| Cap rate | Approximate time to repay the goodwill |
|---|---|
| **2 times** | **~3.5 years** |
| **2.5 times** | **~5 years** |
| **3 times** | **~6 years** |

**Be very wary of a headline multiplier without understanding the repayment period behind it.** A three-times cap rate means six years of post-tax cash before you own anything.

In the worked example, a properly constructed cap rate came out at **2.13**.

**If you are facing a valuation, get independent advice on whether the number makes sense.** The multiplier is the part that looks objective and is not.

## 4. The dilution problem

**This is why so many small firms stay small, and it is worth understanding before you promise anyone equity.**

**Before:**

| | |
|---|---|
| Firm profit | $500,000 |
| Owners | 2 |
| **Profit per principal** | **$250,000** |

**After admitting an associate:**

| | |
|---|---|
| Firm profit | $500,000 |
| Add back the associate's salary | $100,000 |
| Total | $600,000 |
| Owners | 3 |
| **Profit per principal** | **$200,000** |

**The incumbents have been paid for their interest.** But the number that gets remembered is that they used to earn $250,000 and now earn $200,000.

**And, as the presenter put it, their spouse will remember it more clearly than they will.**

**That dilutive effect of an additional snout in the trough is the structural obstacle to succession in small firms.** Recognise it before you start the conversation, not during it.

## 5. Lockstep - how it actually works

The model larger firms and punchier small firms use to get around the dilution problem.

### 5.1 The capital contribution

To come into ownership in a lockstep or no-goodwill firm, the incoming partner makes a **contribution to working capital** - typically **$150,000 to $300,000** tipped into the firm's accounts.

**That is your ticket to get on the ride.** You put it in, and when you leave that is all you get back. **There is no asset growing that is ultimately saleable.**

### 5.2 The points mechanic

A lockstep is about **how profits are shared**, expressed in points or shares. A typical progression: **50, 60, 70, 80, 90, 100**.

**Worked example.**

| | |
|---|---|
| Firm profit | $500,000 |
| Two incumbent partners | 100 points each = 200 |
| New partner | 50 points |
| **Total points** | **250** |
| **Value per point** | **$2,000** |
| New partner receives | 50 × $2,000 = **$100,000** |
| Each incumbent receives | 100 × $2,000 = **$200,000** |

### 5.3 The growth mechanism

**The object is to preserve the value of a profit point.**

In year two the new partner moves to 60 points, so total points become 260. **To hold the point value at $2,000, the firm needs to grow profit by $20,000.**

**That is the elegance of the model.** Progression is funded by growth, not by taking it off the incumbents. And what the incoming partner is really paying for is **foregone profit income** during the ramp.

### 5.4 Performance gates

A straight-line lockstep is the basic version. **Most firms insert performance gates.**

Typically: automatic progression to a point - say 70 - then a gate. **Are you contributing at an appropriate level?** Then possibly a second gate at 90, and then full equity.

### 5.5 Why firms prefer it

- **It is easy for people to come in.** No borrowing, no goodwill purchase, no dilution fight.
- **It lets you manage your equity better**, because the incoming partner has not actually purchased an interest.

**The contrast matters.** If someone has bought a one-third share in a firm on the traditional model, **moving them onto a differential profit share afterwards is awkward at best.** They own a third. A lockstep partner owns points, which can be adjusted.

## 6. Defining partner performance

### 6.1 The counterintuitive recommendation

**In most small firms, do not measure partner performance.**

Two reasons:

**It is very difficult to capture all aspects of partner performance accurately** in a small firm.

**And more importantly, you get pigeonholed.** In a small firm you are put into particular areas of law. **You cannot always do the area that is profitable, or the area that is fun.** Measuring performance in those conditions measures your allocation, not your ability.

### 6.2 What to have instead

**Minimum criteria.** A rough number of hours a day you will turn up and work. Perhaps some minimum billings.

**That is a floor, consistent with the approach to employed lawyers in the Practice Management guide: a hard floor beats a lofty ceiling.**

## 7. When equal shares stop working

### 7.1 The equity equation

Partners come to this conversation because equal profit shares are "no longer working". What is actually happening is that someone is comparing:

> **My compensation ÷ My contribution** against **Their compensation ÷ Their contribution**

**If someone believes their own contribution is stellar and the ratio is out of whack, the obvious move is to increase their own compensation.** Which necessarily reduces someone else's. The alternative framing - lift the others' contribution - is rarely where the conversation goes.

### 7.2 The harder question: what is contribution?

**It is not easy to define.** There are two classes of measure.

| **Objective** | **Subjective** |
|---|---|
| Hours | What you are like with staff |
| Dollars | What you are like with clients |
| Write-offs, realisation, debtors | The **X factor** you bring to the firm - are you a good contributor, or fairly malignant? |

**The observed correlation:** people who are good at the objective measures tend also to be good at the subjective ones. The middle grades may swap around. **And people who perform badly on the objective measures tend to be bad at the subjective ones too.**

### 7.3 The grading, and the sub-types

Grade partners A through D across both dimensions. **But the sub-types are where the useful diagnosis is.**

| Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **A1 - the golden children** | They have caught every ball thrown at them. Clients love them. Staff love them. They are brilliant at what they do |
| **A2 - the angry overachievers** | Usually litigators. **They just want to win. They do not care how much they earn as long as it is more than anyone else** |
| **D1 - the temporary underperformer** | Something largely outside their control has derailed them. The stroke of a government pen has obliterated an area of law. A spouse has terminal cancer |
| **D2 - the permanent underperformer** | **They should not have been made an owner in the first place.** They have somehow slipped through |

### 7.4 The decision rule

**Only change the compensation structure to satisfy an A1 or an A2.**

**And understand what you are risking.** Any change in compensation **can be the beginning of the end for a smaller firm**.

### 7.5 The D1 / D2 distinction is the one to get right

**The consequences are completely different.**

A D1 needs support, time, and a reallocation of work while they recover. Treating a D1 as a D2 destroys a partner who would have come back, and it tells every other partner what happens to them if life goes wrong.

A D2 is a structural problem that will not resolve. **The honest conversation is about exit, not about compensation.**

**Diagnose before you act.**

## 8. Compensation options

### 8.1 The two positions

| **Equality** | **Differential** |
|---|---|
| The default for most small firms. Pure risk sharing. Variance evens out over time | Requires you to define and measure contribution |

**Before moving to differential, understand the motivation for it.** Is this an A1 who genuinely carries the firm, or an A2 who simply wants to earn more than everyone else? **Those require different responses, and only one of them is a business problem.**

### 8.2 The three mechanisms

**1. The 100-point partner.** Each partner holds points and profit is divided by the point pool, as in the lockstep model. Adjustments are made by moving points rather than by renegotiating shares. Flexible, and adjustable without anyone selling an interest.

**2. Differential salaries.** Partners draw different salaries, with profit shared equally or on another basis. **The differential sits in the salary layer rather than in the ownership layer**, which keeps the equity structure intact and keeps the risk-sharing benefit.

**3. High-performance profit pool.** Base profit is shared equally, and a defined pool is set aside and distributed on performance. **This is generally the least disruptive option**, because it leaves the underlying equal share undisturbed and puts only a bounded amount at risk.

### 8.3 The principle to hold

**Whatever you do, remember why you are in partnership.** Every increment of differential is an increment of risk you have taken back onto your own shoulders. Make sure the problem you are solving is worth it.

## 9. Exit

### 9.1 The two patterns

| | **Larger and corporate firms** | **Smaller firms** |
|---|---|---|
| **Typical basis** | Lockstep or no-goodwill. **You get back your capital contribution and nothing more** | Goodwill-based. The outgoing partner's interest must be valued and paid out |

### 9.2 Why exit is the hardest part in a small firm

**Everything in section 4 runs in reverse.** The remaining partners must fund a payout, from a firm whose profit does not increase merely because someone left. And the valuation argument happens at the worst possible time, when the relationship is already ending.

### 9.3 The tax treatment

**Work in progress is usually the largest number in the calculation, and the tax treatment materially changes what each side nets.**

| | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Sale of WIP** | **Assessable income** |
| **Payout to an outgoing partner calculated by reference to WIP** | **Assessable income** |
| **Purchase of WIP** | **Deduction allowed** |

See the **Tax and Accounting** guide. **Model the after-tax position before agreeing a number**, because the headline figure and the net figure can differ substantially.

### 9.4 Plan the exit before you need to

The point made in the risk management session, and it belongs here.

**Ask yourself now: when will you leave practice? What does that look like? Visualise it.** Review it annually; it may change.

**If you are in a partnership, keep those discussions going.** What you cannot afford is a key partner announcing a three-month exit when there is no succession plan and no funding mechanism, leaving the firm high and dry.

**And note the connection to firm value.** A firm whose goodwill depends on one person's relationships has less goodwill than it thinks. The three horizons framework in the **Practice Management** guide puts succession squarely in Horizon 3 for exactly this reason.

### 9.5 Client and file consequences

**Transfer of a practice, or of any part of it, triggers obligations** under [Solicitors Rules r 6](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0245#sec.6):

- **At least 14 days' written notice** to each client before delivering possession of documents, and notice of the client's right to direct otherwise
- **Where trust money is held**, the notice must state the balance, your intention to transfer the account, and the client's right to direct otherwise

**These do not apply where a new partner is simply admitted to a continuing partnership.**

**And a final trust point.** If the practice ceases to hold a trust account, a **final external examination** is required covering each relevant period, lodged **60 days** from the end of the period examined ([LPUGR r 68](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/sl-2015-0246#sec.68)). See the **Trust Accounting** guide.

## 10. Management and governance

### 10.1 Management structure

**Every firm needs a managing partner, including a firm of one.** The full job description is in the **Practice Management** guide at section 6.

In a partnership, the questions to settle explicitly are:

- **Who decides what**, and at what threshold
- **How disputes are resolved** when partners disagree
- **What is reserved to the partnership** as a whole

### 10.2 The silo problem

**Working in silos happens in a two-partner firm as readily as in a corporation.** Both partners effectively run their own practices, with very little cross-discussion about financials, policies, procedures or how each team operates.

**That is a recognised characteristic of claims-prone practices.** See the **Risk Management** guide at section 9.1, and the trust accounting authorities in the **Trust Accounting** guide at section 3.4, which are unanimous that the existence of a system does not relieve a partner of personal vigilance and that verbal assurances from a fellow partner are not enough.

### 10.3 The culture point

**If your firm has a culture of delicacy** - where the partner who runs the trust account does not want to share it, and asking is treated as an accusation - **you have a problem**.

You need a partnership in which transparency is expected and asking questions carries no social cost. **You are as responsible for the trust account, and for everything else, as every other partner.**

## 11. Checklist

**Before entering a partnership**

- [ ] Understand which model you are joining: **goodwill purchase** or **lockstep**
- [ ] If goodwill: does the firm generate profit **beyond a fair market salary to the owners**? If not, you are buying a job
- [ ] Work out the **repayment period** behind any cap rate, not just the multiplier
- [ ] Get independent advice on the valuation
- [ ] If lockstep: understand that your capital contribution is a ticket, not an asset
- [ ] Understand the **performance gates** and what triggers them
- [ ] Model the **after-tax** position, including WIP

**Before admitting a partner**

- [ ] Model the **dilution** honestly and have the conversation about it before you make the offer
- [ ] Decide whether goodwill purchase or lockstep suits your firm's growth path
- [ ] Set the performance gates
- [ ] Agree the working capital contribution

**Running the partnership**

- [ ] Set **minimum criteria** for partners rather than attempting full performance measurement
- [ ] Keep the financials, policies and procedures visible to every partner. **Do not silo**
- [ ] Hold partners accountable for supervision and management, not just billings
- [ ] Diagnose D1 versus D2 before acting on any underperformance
- [ ] Before changing compensation, ask whether the person driving it is an A1 or an A2
- [ ] If you must go differential, prefer the option that leaves the underlying equal share intact

**Exit and succession**

- [ ] Answer the question now: when will you leave, and what does it look like?
- [ ] Review it annually and keep the partnership conversation live
- [ ] Ensure the partnership agreement sets out valuation methodology, payout terms and funding
- [ ] Model the tax treatment of WIP on exit
- [ ] Know the r 6 client notification obligations and the final external examination requirement
