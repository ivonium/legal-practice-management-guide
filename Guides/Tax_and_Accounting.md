# Tax and Accounting - Practical Guide

Taxation and financial matters for legal practices and for you personally. Stated as at 8 September 2026, for the 2026-27 financial year.

**This is a working summary, not tax advice.** The rules change constantly, the ATO's positions on professional firms are under active review, and almost every question below has a "depends on your circumstances" layer. Use it to know what to ask your accountant, and to spot the issues before they become problems.

---

## 1. The headline

**Tax imposes a great deal of compliance obligation on a law practice, and a small firm faces substantially the same obligations as a large one - just at a different scale.**

What that requires is **a disciplined approach to quarterly accounts, tax matters and cash flow**. Nothing clever. Just doing it on time, every quarter.

### 1.1 What has changed recently

The single most operationally significant change for anyone setting up now:

| Change | When | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Payday Super** | **1 July 2026** | Employers must pay superannuation guarantee **within 7 business days of each payday**, not quarterly |
| **Maximum contribution base moves to annual** | 1 July 2026 | **$270,830** for 2026-27, replacing the per-quarter figure |
| **"Qualifying earnings" replaces "ordinary time earnings"** | 1 July 2026 | New base for calculating SG |
| **Concessional contributions cap** | 1 July 2026 | Up to **$32,500** from $30,000 |
| **Non-concessional cap** | 1 July 2026 | Up to **$130,000** from $120,000; bring-forward maximum **$390,000** |
| **Division 296** | 1 July 2026 | Additional 15% tax on realised super earnings for the portion of a balance between **$3m and $10m**, and a further 10% above $10m |
| **PCG 2025/5** | Current | New ATO risk assessment framework for personal services income |

**Note on Payday Super.** The Super Guarantee Charge for late payment **is not tax deductible**, and the ATO now has near-real-time visibility through Single Touch Payroll. The old practice of running behind on super is over.

## 2. Part 1: Registration and the compliance load

### 2.1 Registration requirements

| Registration | When |
|---|---|
| **Tax File Number (TFN)** | For any new entity. If you run a sole proprietorship in your own name with a personal ABN, you use your **personal TFN** - which means all your tax data sits with you |
| **Australian Business Number (ABN)** | **Any business**, including sole practitioners. You cannot trade without it |
| **GST** | **Compulsory if turnover exceeds $75,000** |

### 2.2 Should you register for GST before you have to?

A genuine decision, not a formality.

**Reasons not to register below the threshold:**

- **Admin.** You avoid preparing a BAS and working out GST payable and receivable.
- **You do not have to charge it.** If you invoice a private individual $1,000 and you are not registered, they pay $1,000. If you are registered, they pay $1,100. **An unregistered practice is effectively 10% cheaper to a non-business client.** For a family lawyer acting for individuals, that can be a real business development point.

**Reasons to register early:**

- **You can claim input tax credits on set-up costs.** If you are incurring significant establishment expenditure, the credits matter.
- **You can backdate the registration.** So the decision is not irreversible.
- **You have four years to claim credits**, so there is time.

**Two warnings.** If you registered before the asset was acquired in a different name, you will need a transaction to move it into the entity to claim the credit. And there is a cautionary example: a sole practitioner whose business development strategy was an extremely expensive fit-out, generating substantial GST credits, but never generating enough revenue to justify it. The practice no longer exists.

**In most cases the costs of a small start-up practice are not large enough for the credits to outweigh the admin.** Weigh it rather than assume.

### 2.3 Accounting for income

| Basis | Income assessable | Deductions allowable |
|---|---|---|
| **Accruals (earnings)** | When it becomes recoverable, that is **when billed** | When the expense is **incurred** - definitively committed |
| **Cash (receipts)** | **When received** | When incurred |

**The general principle: if you are in business, you record income on an earnings basis.** You raise an invoice, it becomes revenue in that year, and you are taxed on it whether or not you have been paid.

**The exception that matters for sole practitioners.** Where you are principally running a sole practice and generating all the revenue yourself, there is a common law principle - which the ATO does adopt - permitting you to **record income on a receipts basis**.

**The distinction is material:** are you paying tax on your debtors, meaning uncollected fees, or paying tax when you actually get paid?

**On the deduction side the treatment is the same either way.** You get the deduction when the cost is incurred - when you have an obligation to pay - provided you have the evidence.

**Work this through with your accountant before the first year closes.**

## 3. GST

### 3.1 The basics

- **10%** of the value of goods and services made as a **taxable supply**
- Taxable supplies are made by registered businesses, which provide a **tax invoice**
- Registered businesses get an **input tax credit** for GST paid on their inputs
- **Sole practitioners must apportion business and private use**

**GST is not an expense.** It goes to the balance sheet, not the profit and loss.

### 3.2 Reporting and basis

| | |
|---|---|
| **Monthly** | If annual turnover exceeds **$20 million**, or by choice |
| **Quarterly** | Otherwise |
| **Cash basis** | By choice if business turnover is under **$10 million** |
| **Accruals basis** | Otherwise |

Accounted for on the **Business Activity Statement (BAS)**.

**The accruals trap: on an accruals basis you pay GST on bills you have raised but not collected.** Watch your debtors. This is one of the specific reasons debtor days matter in a law practice, and it ties directly to the cash flow material in the **Practice Management** guide.

### 3.3 Exports of services

The general exception: **if you supply services to an overseas client who is offshore when they receive the advice, that is an export of your service and GST is not charged.**

### 3.4 What "tax invoice" means

When the words **tax invoice** appear on an invoice, it implies there is GST in the fee. Same when you receive one from a supplier - that is the document supporting your input tax credit.

**Keep them.** The ATO can and does email asking you to produce the invoices supporting your credits. That happens most often in **the first two or three quarters of a new business**, and the ATO will also use it to check you are genuinely carrying on a business.

### 3.5 Private expenses

There is no reason your firm cannot pay private costs - your accountant will treat them appropriately for tax. But be clear that:

- **You do not get a GST credit for anything private**
- **You do not get a tax deduction either**, unless you pay FBT on it

## 4. Disbursements

**The single most law-specific GST issue, and the one most frequently got wrong.**

The GST treatment depends entirely on the **contractual arrangement**: who is the contracting party.

### 4.1 The two categories

| | **Paid as agent for the client** | **Incurred by the solicitor** |
|---|---|---|
| **Contracting party** | The **client** | The **solicitor** |
| **GST on payment** | **None payable** | Payable |
| **GST credit** | **None** (even if GST was paid) | **Solicitor may claim the credit** |
| **On reimbursement** | Not your transaction at all | **GST is payable** on reimbursement by the client |
| **Typically runs through** | The **trust account** | The office account and your books |

### 4.2 Agency disbursements - typical examples

Where the client is engaged and you are simply the paying agent:

- **Fines, penalties, stamp duty and taxes**
- **Application fees**
- **Court fees**
- **Registration fees**

### 4.3 Solicitor-incurred disbursements - typical examples

Where you are the contracting party:

- **Barrister's fees** where **the barrister is engaged by the solicitor** - which is almost always the case, partly because they have to be and partly because the barrister wants to be paid by you, not your client
- **Witness fees**
- **Search fees** - where the firm has contracted with the search provider, this cannot realistically be put on an agency basis
- **Postage, photocopying, couriers, travel** - these attract GST on payment by the client

### 4.4 The barrister worked example

**Solicitor-incurred basis:**

1. Barrister invoices the law firm: **$100 plus $10 GST = $110**
2. Law firm pays $110 and **claims the $10 GST credit** - so it is out of pocket $100 net
3. Law firm charges the client **$100 plus $10 GST = $110**
4. **Net GST effect on the firm: nil.** But **every one of those transactions goes through your books and your BAS.** That is the admin cost.

**Agency basis** (if the provider did not need to be engaged by the firm):

- **None of those transactions are yours.** It is a transaction between the service provider and the client.
- The invoice is from the service provider **to your client**, even though you may pass the cash or the invoice through.
- Your only administrative involvement is money moving through your trust account.

### 4.5 The practical rule

**For a small firm, use the agency basis wherever you can.** It removes the admin entirely.

**Where it matters most: fees with no GST on them.** Court filing fees and some searches carry no GST. If the firm is registered and you are **not** on an agency basis, you pay $100 with no GST and then have to **charge GST on it to your client** - so the client ends up paying GST on something that never attracted GST. **Put those on an agency basis if at all possible.**

### 4.6 What determines which basis applies

**Your costs agreement.** The basis is set by how your engagement with the client is structured. So:

- **Know what your costs agreement says**
- **Make sure the client is clear on how it works**
- **Make sure whoever prepares your books understands the treatment**

### 4.7 The consequence of getting it wrong

Inconsistent treatment between the parties' books and records means somebody ends up out of pocket - **and it is usually the client**. Typical failures: the client charged GST because the firm forgot to claim the credit; or GST that never gets passed on.

**Record keeping on disbursements is consistently poor across the profession.** Modern practice management systems, set up properly, handle this well. Set them up properly.

### 4.8 The trust account connection

Agency disbursements move **in and out of your trust account** without touching your books and records. No GST accounting is required on them because they are not your transactions.

**The trust accounting obligations are unaffected.** See the **Trust Accounting** guide, and note in particular:

- Trust money taken up front for disbursements is trust money.
- **Rule 42 Method 3** lets you reimburse yourself from trust for a disbursement **already paid** - meaning your office account has actually been debited. You cannot draw from trust first and then pay.
- A cheque payable to Revenue NSW for duty, received from the client, is **transit money**.

## 5. PAYG

### 5.1 Two branches

| Branch | What it is |
|---|---|
| **PAYG withholding** | Someone else's tax. Tax you deduct from payments you make, principally salary and wages to employees |
| **PAYG instalments** | Your own income tax, paid quarterly as you go |

**Both are recorded on the BAS**, and both apply to companies and sole practitioners.

**If you become a partner in a partnership, you move into the quarterly instalment system.**

### 5.2 Single Touch Payroll

**Payroll is now digitally connected to the ATO.** If you run a payroll of any kind, including paying yourself, you must be signed up to **Single Touch Payroll**. The ATO sees every payroll movement.

It is tedious to set up and relatively straightforward once running. Modern systems handle it.

### 5.3 PAYG instalments

- You can pay a **calculated instalment** or the amount advised by the ATO.
- The calculated instalment is **instalment income × instalment rate**, so it varies with your turnover for the quarter.
- Payments are credited against your tax liability on your return.

**The timing trap, and it is a serious one for a new practice.** For the **June 2027 year**, no tax is paid until **March or May 2028**.

**That means you can operate for well over a year before your first real tax bill arrives.** Plan for it. Put money aside from day one. The number of practitioners who are caught by this is the reason it gets its own slide.

## 6. Superannuation

### 6.1 The employer obligation

| | |
|---|---|
| **Superannuation Guarantee rate** | **12%** (settled; no further increase scheduled) |
| **Deductibility** | **100% deductible** |
| **Payment timing** | Under **Payday Super**, within **7 business days of each payday** |
| **Maximum contribution base 2026-27** | **$270,830** annually (previously a quarterly figure) |
| **Base for calculation** | **Qualifying earnings** (replacing ordinary time earnings) |

**The progression tells you where this is heading.** Super obligations moved from annual to quarterly to monthly to same-pay-cycle, because firms were effectively financing themselves on unpaid super. With STP feeding the ATO in real time, **there is no room to run behind any more**, and the Super Guarantee Charge for late payment **is not deductible**.

### 6.2 The deduction timing rule

**Superannuation falls outside the general "incurred" principle.** You do not get the deduction until the money is **in the super fund**.

**Practical consequence: if you pay on 30 June it will not land in the fund by 30 June, so the deduction moves to the following year.** Pay super obligations **a few weeks before 30 June** if you want the deduction in that year.

### 6.3 Contribution caps for you personally

| | 2026-27 |
|---|---|
| **Concessional (before tax)** | **$32,500** - includes employer SG, salary sacrifice and personal deductible contributions combined |
| **Non-concessional (after tax)** | **$130,000**, conditions apply |
| **Bring-forward maximum** | **$390,000** under 75, tiered by total super balance |

**Tax deductible super contributions are available to the self-employed and to employees.** You can claim them personally on your return, or through the firm as salary sacrifice.

**Carry-forward.** If your total super balance is under **$500,000**, you can carry forward unused concessional cap amounts from the previous **five years**. In a high-income year that is potentially a substantial deduction.

**The arithmetic that makes this the strongest personal lever.** Concessional contributions are taxed at **15%** in the fund (or 30% if Division 293 applies) against a marginal rate of up to 47%.

**Watch the interaction.** Compulsory employer SG at the maximum contribution base produces $32,499.60 - deliberately just under the $32,500 cap. On a high salary, SG alone can fill the entire cap, leaving no room for salary sacrifice or personal deductible contributions.

### 6.4 No salary, no compulsory super

**Compulsory super applies only to salary and wages** (qualifying earnings). If you are not paying yourself a salary, there is no compulsory super obligation. You can still make a personal contribution.

## 7. Fringe Benefits Tax

| | |
|---|---|
| **What it taxes** | Benefits provided to **employees only** |
| **Rate** | **47%** (year ending 31 March 2027) |
| **Common benefits** | Cars, expense payments, **entertainment** |
| **Reported on** | Forms part of the BAS |

### 7.1 Why FBT is largely a thing of the past

**Firms generally do not provide fringe benefits to employed lawyers.** And economically there is little point: whether you pay $100 of fringe benefit or $100 of salary, the outcome is broadly the same, so you may as well pay the salary and withhold the tax.

**FBT does not apply to partners**, because a partner is not an employee.

### 7.2 Where it still bites: entertainment

**If you run an incorporated practice and pay for meals, coffees and entertainment, you have to work out whether it is a non-deductible expense to the firm, or a deductible expense subject to FBT.**

**Exceptions:**

- **In-house conferences** with entertainment provided - fine
- **Overseas conferences** that are genuinely mostly conference - fine
- Entertainment **attached to a training event** or provided **in house** - fine
- **Coffee** - generally fine, deemed immaterial

**But generally, if you step out of the office into a restaurant for a meal, there are tax consequences.** Not large dollars individually, but they accumulate, and the rules around entertainment are extensive. Most people structure to stay out of it.

### 7.3 Tools of trade - the exemption that matters

**Work-related items are tax deductible and no FBT applies.** Devices, subscriptions, an iPad, a pen. If it fits that category you do not need to think about FBT.

### 7.4 Electric vehicles

An FBT exemption has been available for EVs where all the conditions are met. Worth checking the current position with your accountant, as the concession has been subject to change.

### 7.5 Not-for-profits

Substantial FBT concessions apply to not-for-profit employers.

## 8. Deductions - the practical questions

### 8.1 The general principle

**If it is fit for purpose and used for work, it does not matter what shape, size or colour it is.** Substance over form.

**The test in practice:** does it go out to dinner with you on a Saturday night? If yes, it is not deductible.

The worked example was a wheelie bag used to carry files between offices and to client meetings - deductible, notwithstanding an eye-watering price tag, because it was demonstrably fit for the work purpose and not used personally. The same bag used as an accessory is not.

**The safest position is 100% business use, or at minimum predominantly business use with the ability to demonstrate it.**

### 8.2 Travel

- **Home to work is never deductible.**
- If you work from home and travel to see clients, **that is deductible**.
- If you have a CBD office, go there, and then travel to a client, **that is deductible**. The home-to-office leg is not.

### 8.3 Conference and CPD travel

**If you keep a travel diary and do work-related things on a trip, that portion is technically deductible.** But **evidence and documentation are essential**, and travel deductions are an area the ATO polices hard.

Attempts to characterise a holiday as a CPD trip are well known and well scrutinised.

### 8.4 Home office

**Rules have tightened since COVID and the dollars do not add up to much.** Worth claiming, not worth planning around.

## 10. Service trusts

A structure with a long history and a great deal of ATO attention.

### 10.1 What it does

- Charges a **mark-up on wages and other costs**
- At **commercially realistic rates**
- Provides **administrative convenience for superannuation**
- Provides the **ability to split income**

The foundational authority is **FCT v Phillips**.

### 10.2 The risk

**Part IVA applies if the dominant purpose is obtaining a tax benefit.**

### 10.3 How to keep it defensible

- The service entity must be a **commercially operated entity responsible for its own functions and risks**
- There must be a **written agreement** setting out the nature of the services provided
- **Fees must be charged at a commercial rate**
- The service trust should **regularly invoice the practice - and actually be paid**
- **Fees must not be grossly excessive**
- The arrangement must accord with **TR 2006/2** and the ATO's *Service Entity Arrangements* guide

### 10.4 Current position

**There is significant ongoing ATO activity in this area.** The relevant material is **IT 276**, **TR 2006/2**, and the ATO's *Your service entity arrangements* guide, which sets out **indicative mark-up rates carrying a low risk of audit**.

**Use the indicative rates.** They exist precisely so that you can structure inside a safe harbour.

## 11. Incorporated legal practices

| | |
|---|---|
| **Governing law** | [Legal Profession Uniform Law (NSW)](https://legislation.nsw.gov.au/view/html/inforce/current/act-2014-16a) |
| **Requirement** | At least **one legal practitioner director** |
| **Notification** | **Must advise the Law Society** |
| **Company tax rate** | **30%**, or **25%** for base rate entities with turnover under $50 million |
| **Income out** | Distributed as **salary or dividends** |
| **Advantages** | External funding; creditor protection |

### 11.1 Getting money out of a company

A live question for anyone setting up as a sole director with no employees.

**Option 1: pay yourself a salary.** Straightforward, but it triggers the full payroll apparatus - PAYG withholding, Single Touch Payroll, and Payday Super within seven business days. That is real administrative overhead and probably another software subscription.

**Option 2: drawings, treated as a loan.** Viable in the early stages, **but you must navigate the shareholder loan rules in Division 7A**. This is not optional and it is not forgiving. Get it right from the start.

**Option 3: dividends.** At some point your accountant will discuss paying dividends against the drawing account. **Dividends do not attract compulsory superannuation.**

**The considered position.** There is nothing wrong with paying yourself a salary from day one. But be aware of the administration attached, and if you are time poor at start-up, deferring it is reasonable - **subject to the ATO guidance point below**.

**The countervailing point.** Under the ATO's professional firm profits guidance, **a certain amount of income needs to be returned in the principal's own name**, because the ATO wants the practitioner doing the legal work to have a meaningful amount of income in their personal return. **Paying yourself a salary is a clean way of satisfying that**, particularly in a firm with multiple principals or partner-equivalents.

**And note the direction of travel.** The answer to these questions five years ago would have been materially different. Obligations are increasing, and they fall hardest on small practices.

## 12. ATO guidance on professional firm profits and trust distributions

Two areas requiring specific attention.

### 12.1 Allocation of professional firm profits

**Practical Compliance Guideline PCG 2021/4** sets out the ATO's approach to the allocation of professional firm profits.

In substance it is a risk-assessment framework. It scores arrangements against benchmarks including the proportion of profit returned in the principal's own name and the effective tax rate on the practitioner's share. Low-risk arrangements are left alone; high-risk arrangements attract attention.

**Structure to the guideline, not around it.**

### 12.2 Trust distributions

**Section 100A**, with **TR 2022/4** and **PCG 2022/2**.

Section 100A concerns reimbursement agreements - broadly, arrangements where a beneficiary is made presently entitled to trust income but the economic benefit flows to someone else. The 2022 ruling and guideline substantially narrowed what the ATO regards as acceptable in family trust distribution arrangements.

**If your structure includes a family trust making discretionary distributions, this needs current review.** The practice of distributing to adult children who never receive the funds is squarely in the ATO's sights.

## 13. Personal services income

### 13.1 What the rules do

**The PSI rules prevent the alienation of personal services income.**

**PSI is income received primarily as a reward for personal skills and effort.**

### 13.2 When the rules apply

**If more than 80% of your PSI comes from one particular client, the PSI rules apply** - unless you satisfy **one of three tests**:

1. **Unrelated clients test**
2. **Employment test**
3. **Business premises test**

**The rules do not apply to a Personal Services Business.**

### 13.3 The current ATO framework - PCG 2025/5

A new risk assessment framework and guideline.

| Risk rating | Position |
|---|---|
| **Low risk** | Do not retain PSI indefinitely or divert it to associates. **PSI taxed at the individual's marginal rate** |
| **High risk** | **Likely to be targeted by the ATO.** Generally where PSI is taxed at a lower rate |

**Two points to note:**

- **Commercial reasons for arrangements are important.** A structure with a genuine commercial rationale is in a different position from one that exists only for the tax outcome.
- **The ATO expects a genuine move to low risk by June 2027.** That is a deadline, not a suggestion. If your structure is currently high risk, you have limited time.

## 14. Work in progress - buying and selling

Relevant whenever a practice changes hands or a partner comes in or out.

| | Treatment |
|---|---|
| **Sale of WIP** | **Assessable income** |
| **Payout to outgoing partners** calculated by reference to WIP | **Assessable income** |
| **Purchase of WIP** | **Deduction allowed** |

**This is a change from the historical position**, under which there was no relief for the purchase of WIP and it formed part of capital costs.

**The practical significance.** WIP is often the largest single number in a partnership admission or retirement calculation, and the tax treatment materially changes what each side actually nets. See the **Partnership Management** guide on entry and exit valuation.

## 15. Cash flow planning

The theme running through the whole session.

**The obligations you must fund:**

| Obligation | Frequency |
|---|---|
| GST on the BAS | Quarterly (or monthly above $20m turnover) |
| PAYG withholding | Per BAS cycle |
| PAYG instalments | Quarterly |
| **Superannuation guarantee** | **Within 7 business days of every payday** |
| FBT | Annually, via the BAS |
| Income tax | Per assessment |

**The three things that catch new practices:**

1. **The first tax bill arrives late and large.** For the June 2027 year, nothing is due until March or May 2028. Put money aside from the first month.
2. **GST on an accruals basis is payable on bills you have not collected.** Your debtor days are a tax cash flow problem, not just a working capital problem.
3. **Payday super removes the float.** Super now leaves the account with every pay run.

**Prepare quarterly accounts.** Not annual. Quarterly, matched to the BAS cycle, so the numbers you file are numbers you have actually looked at.

## 16. Checklist

**Setting up**

- [ ] Decide the structure before you register anything - and take advice on asset protection, not just tax
- [ ] Apply for the entity's TFN and ABN
- [ ] Decide on GST registration: do the arithmetic on set-up credits against admin and the 10% client-facing difference
- [ ] Confirm with your accountant whether you are on an **accruals or receipts** basis for income
- [ ] Register for Single Touch Payroll if you will pay any salary, including your own
- [ ] Choose an accounting package that integrates with your practice management system. **Do not run this on a spreadsheet**

**Disbursements**

- [ ] Review your costs agreement to establish which disbursements are on an **agency** basis
- [ ] Put court fees, filing fees, stamp duty and registration fees on an agency basis wherever possible
- [ ] Make sure whoever keeps your books understands the distinction
- [ ] Make sure the client understands it
- [ ] Check your system is coding GST-free disbursements correctly

**Ongoing**

- [ ] Prepare quarterly accounts, not just an annual set
- [ ] Keep tax invoices for every input tax credit claimed
- [ ] Pay super **within 7 business days of every payday**
- [ ] Pay any 30 June super obligations **a few weeks early** so they land in the fund
- [ ] Set money aside for the first tax bill from day one
- [ ] Track debtor days - they are a GST cash flow issue on accruals
- [ ] Keep a travel diary if you intend to claim any travel

**Annually or on change**

- [ ] Review your structure against **PCG 2021/4** (professional firm profits) and **PCG 2025/5** (PSI)
- [ ] If you have a family trust, review distributions against **s 100A**, **TR 2022/4** and **PCG 2022/2**
- [ ] If you have a service trust, confirm the written agreement exists, fees are commercial, invoices are issued **and paid**, and the mark-up sits within the ATO's indicative rates
- [ ] Check your concessional cap position and any carry-forward capacity before 30 June
- [ ] If you are high risk on PSI, move to low risk **before June 2027**
